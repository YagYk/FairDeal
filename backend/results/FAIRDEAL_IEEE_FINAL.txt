ABSTRACT  [REPLACE THE ENTIRE EXISTING ABSTRACT WITH THIS]
================================================================================


Abstract— Evaluating employment contracts requires legal expertise that most Indian job seekers lack, and existing legal AI tools target Western jurisdictions without addressing the informal language, hybrid legal–HR terminology, and regional variability found in Indian offer letters. This paper presents FAIRDEAL, an open-source contract analysis platform that combines hybrid field extraction (93 regex patterns with LLM-based fallback), statistical market benchmarking against 21 curated datasets, and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) over 60 indexed contracts to produce fairness scores and negotiation playbooks. At its core, a five-dimensional psychological scoring engine evaluates salary, notice period, benefits, restrictive clauses, and legal compliance through dynamic weighting and context multipliers. A systematic evaluation on 30 contracts across four company categories yields a 100% scoring pass rate, 100% known-ordering compliance on 15 unambiguous contract pairs, and 100% determinism across repeated runs. Feature–score correlation analysis confirms strong alignment between salary percentile (r = 0.934) and benefits count (r = 0.943) with final scores, while inter-category Welch's t-tests demonstrate statistically significant discrimination between product and service companies (t = 10.79, Cohen's d = 4.60, p < 0.05). A pilot study on six real Indian employment contracts achieves full extraction accuracy for salary and probation fields. To our knowledge, FAIRDEAL is the first open-source platform to deliver automated contract valuation, statistical benchmarking, and negotiation intelligence specifically designed for Indian employment law.

I. INTRODUCTION  [REPLACE THE ENTIRE INTRODUCTION WITH THIS]
================================================================================

India's formal employment sector produces millions of offer letters every year, yet fewer than five percent of candidates seek professional advice before signing [5]. This information gap is most acute among entry-level technology hires, where restrictive clauses such as two-year training bonds, 90-day notice periods, and broad intellectual-property assignments are standard practice. Although Legal NLP research has advanced rapidly—delivering domain-specific language models [1], benchmark datasets [4], and retrieval-augmented frameworks [8]—the resulting tools remain concentrated on Western corporate law. Indian employment contracts differ structurally: salary figures appear as "18 LPA," "Rs. 1,800,000/-," or "Cost to Company: 18 lakhs per annum" within a single industry; notice-period terms range from explicit day counts to implicit "refer to HR policy" references; and statutory obligations under the Code on Wages, 2019 [16] coexist with company-specific clauses that no public dataset covers.

FAIRDEAL bridges this gap by providing an end-to-end, open-source platform that converts raw offer letters into quantified fairness assessments and actionable negotiation recommendations. The system accepts PDF and DOCX documents—including scanned pages handled through a multi-tier OCR pipeline—and passes them through an eight-stage analysis workflow that includes hybrid extraction, statistical benchmarking, five-dimensional psychological scoring, and retrieval-augmented evidence generation.

The key contributions of this paper are:

1) A hybrid extraction pipeline combining 93 deterministic regex patterns with LLM-based fallback, supporting the diverse salary and clause formats found in Indian employment contracts.

2) A five-dimensional psychological scoring engine with dynamic weights and context multipliers, validated on 30 contracts with 100% pass rate and 100% known-ordering compliance across 15 unambiguous contract pairs.

3) A comparative RAG system over 60 indexed contracts stored in ChromaDB, enabling clause-level similarity retrieval for evidence-based insights.

4) An end-to-end platform with context-aware negotiation playbook generation tailored to Indian employment law, company type, and role level.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews related work. Section III describes the system architecture. Section IV details the methodology and implementation. Section V presents the experimental evaluation. Section VI discusses findings and limitations. Section VII concludes with future directions.

II. RELATED WORK  [REPLACE THE ENTIRE RELATED WORK WITH THIS]
================================================================================

Legal NLP has matured considerably over the past five years. Chalkidis et al. [1] introduced LEGAL-BERT, a transformer model pre-trained on 12 GB of legal text that set new benchmarks on contract clause classification tasks. While LEGAL-BERT excels at categorizing legal provisions, it does not extract structured fields, compute market-relative percentiles, or produce negotiation guidance—capabilities that FAIRDEAL targets.

From a fairness-theory perspective, Anderson et al. [2] formalized employment-contract fairness as a statistical distribution rather than a binary label, arguing that a contract can be "fair for the 30th percentile" but unfair at the 70th. FAIRDEAL operationalizes this insight through percentile-based scoring against curated market data, translating theoretical fairness into a quantitative score.

Commercial platforms such as SpeedLegal provide automated clause highlighting, but Kerikmäe et al. [3] observed that these tools function primarily as risk detectors: they flag problematic terms but do not compare them against market norms or generate proactive negotiation strategies. Surden [8] echoed this limitation, noting that most legal AI systems serve compliance professionals rather than the individuals who sign the contracts.

The CUAD dataset released by Hendrycks et al. [4]—41 clause types across 510 U.S. commercial contracts—remains the most widely used benchmark for legal AI. However, its exclusive focus on American corporate law limits direct applicability to Indian employment contexts. Sharma et al. [5] documented how Indian employment contracts exhibit informal language, regional variability, and hybrid terminology that falls outside CUAD's annotation schema, underscoring the need for jurisdiction-specific solutions.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), introduced by Lewis et al. [9], has demonstrated strong performance on knowledge-intensive NLP tasks by grounding LLM outputs in retrieved evidence. FAIRDEAL adapts this paradigm for legal analysis: contract clauses are embedded using sentence-transformers [14] and searched against a vector store of 60 indexed contracts, enabling evidence-based comparison rather than unconstrained generation.

V. EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION

A. Evaluation Dataset and Methodology

To validate FAIRDEAL's scoring and extraction pipeline in a controlled and reproducible manner, a test corpus of 30 employment contracts was constructed. Each contract was authored with predetermined ground-truth values for all critical fields—annual CTC, notice period, bond amount, non-compete duration, probation period, and benefits—so that the expected fairness score range could be computed independently of the system under test. The 30 contracts span four company categories: Service (11 contracts, CTC range 3.5–5.0 lakhs), Product (11 contracts, CTC range 8.0–30.0 lakhs), Startup (6 contracts, CTC range 10.0–24.0 lakhs), and Consulting (2 contracts, CTC range 7.5–8.5 lakhs). This distribution mirrors the Indian technology employment landscape, where service companies dominate entry-level hiring and product companies offer premium packages.

Additionally, six real anonymized contracts from major Indian employers were retained for qualitative extraction validation. These real contracts were not used for scoring validation to avoid circularity between extraction errors and score errors.

The evaluation addresses three research questions:
  RQ1: Does the scoring engine produce valid and consistent fairness scores?
  RQ2: Can the engine differentiate meaningfully between contract quality tiers?
  RQ3: Do input features correlate with output scores in domain-expected directions?

B. Scoring Engine Validation (RQ1)

Each of the 30 test contracts was assigned an expected score range based on its ground-truth field values and the known behavior of the scoring formula. The scoring engine was then executed on all 30 inputs, and the resulting fairness scores were compared against expected ranges.

Results: All 30 scores fell within their expected ranges, yielding a 100% pass rate. To verify determinism, each test case was evaluated three times with identical inputs; all runs produced identical scores, confirming 100% determinism. Furthermore, 15 contract pairs with unambiguous quality orderings were defined (e.g., Salesforce at 30 LPA with equity and no restrictions versus TCS at 3.6 LPA with a two-year bond and 90-day notice). The scoring engine correctly ranked all 15 pairs, achieving 100% known-ordering compliance.

C. Inter-Category Discrimination (RQ2)

A critical requirement for any scoring system is the ability to distinguish between objectively different quality levels. Category-level score statistics and pairwise Welch's t-tests were computed.

Product companies achieved a mean score of 88.36 (SD = 6.64), followed by startups at 87.50 (SD = 6.77), consulting at 72.50 (SD = 2.12), and service companies at 60.36 (SD = 5.48). These orderings are consistent with domain expectations: product companies in India typically offer higher compensation, shorter notice periods, and fewer restrictive clauses than service companies.

E. Component Score Analysis

The five scoring dimensions—Salary, Notice, Benefits, Clauses, and Legal Compliance—were analyzed independently across all 30 contracts to characterize their individual contributions.

Mean component scores are: Salary 61.0 (SD = 26.4), Notice 70.1 (SD = 15.5), Benefits 90.7 (SD = 7.9), Clauses 95.9 (SD = 6.0), and Legal 100.0 (SD = 0.0). The high variance in Salary scores reflects the deliberate wide CTC range (3.5–30.0 LPA) in the test corpus, while the uniformly perfect Legal scores indicate that all test contracts satisfied basic Indian statutory requirements.

F. Field Extraction Performance

On the six real anonymized contracts, regex-only extraction achieved the following accuracy rates: CTC 100% (6/6), notice period 83% (5/6), bond amount 80% (4/5 applicable), and probation period 100% (6/6). The single notice-period failure occurred in a contract that used the phrase "as per company policy" without specifying a day count. In such cases, the hybrid pipeline triggers LLM-based extraction (SniperExtractionService), which successfully surfaced the implicit reference, ensuring the user is informed of the ambiguity.

Deterministic extraction latency—measured for the regex layer only, excluding LLM, RAG, scoring, and narration—averaged below 100 ms per contract. The full end-to-end analysis pipeline, including all eight stages, completes in 3–8 seconds depending on whether LLM-based extraction or narration is triggered.

G. Qualitative Robustness

The extraction pipeline demonstrated resilience to diverse formatting variations encountered in real Indian contracts. Salary expressions such as "6.06 LPA," "Rs. 4.75 Lacs," and "₹18,00,000" were correctly normalized to comparable INR values. Notice-period clauses phrased as "60 days' advance notice" and "serve 30 days' notice" were parsed accurately despite punctuation and sentence-structure variability. Bond-related obligations expressed using alternative terminology—including "service agreement," "liquidated damages," and "minimum service commitment"—were correctly identified beyond standard "training bond" phrasing.

VI. DISCUSSION

The experimental results provide evidence that FAIRDEAL's scoring engine produces valid, consistent, and well-differentiated fairness scores. The 100% pass rate across 30 contracts with predetermined score ranges, combined with perfect known-ordering compliance and determinism, demonstrates that the scoring formula behaves predictably and correctly under controlled conditions.

The inter-category discrimination results are particularly noteworthy. Cohen's d values exceeding 4.0 for the product-vs-service and startup-vs-service comparisons represent very large effect sizes, meaning the score separation between categories is not merely statistically significant but practically meaningful. A hiring candidate receiving a score of 88 (product mean) versus 60 (service mean) obtains a clear, quantified signal about relative contract quality—precisely the kind of information that supports informed decision-making.

The feature-correlation analysis validates the internal logic of the scoring engine. The strong positive correlations for salary (r = 0.934) and benefits (r = 0.943) confirm that the two most impactful contract dimensions drive scores as intended. The strong negative correlation for notice percentile (r = −0.753) correctly reflects that longer notice periods restrict employee mobility. The weak positive non-compete correlation (r = 0.157) arises from confounding—premium-CTC companies that impose non-competes also score highly on salary and benefits—and represents a known trade-off rather than a defect. Future work could explore interaction terms to disentangle this effect.

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the 30-contract test corpus was synthetically constructed; while it enables precise ground-truth validation, it does not capture the full variability of real-world documents. The six-contract pilot partially addresses this gap but remains small. A larger annotated corpus of real Indian contracts would strengthen generalizability claims. Second, the Shapiro–Wilk test (p = 0.034) indicates slight non-normality in the score distribution, attributable to the inherent bimodality between low-CTC service contracts and high-CTC product/startup contracts. This bimodality reflects a genuine feature of the Indian employment market rather than a scoring artifact. Third, the system currently supports only English-language contracts, and OCR accuracy degrades for handwritten annotations and extremely low-quality scans. Fourth, market benchmarking precision may decrease for niche roles where curated datasets are sparse.

VII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

This paper presented FAIRDEAL, an open-source contract analysis platform designed specifically for Indian employment contexts. The system integrates hybrid extraction (93 regex patterns with LLM fallback), statistical market benchmarking across 21 curated datasets, retrieval-augmented generation over 60 indexed contracts, and a five-dimensional psychological scoring engine to deliver fairness assessments and negotiation playbooks.

Systematic evaluation on 30 contracts demonstrated 100% scoring pass rate, 100% ordering compliance across 15 contract pairs, and 100% determinism. Feature–score correlations confirmed that salary (r = 0.934) and benefits (r = 0.943) are the dominant fairness drivers, while inter-category t-tests established statistically significant discrimination between product and service companies (Cohen's d = 4.60). A pilot study on six real contracts validated extraction accuracy across key fields.

Several directions for future work are planned. First, multi-language support will be introduced to parse Hindi, Telugu, Tamil, and Bengali contracts, which are prevalent in non-metropolitan Indian employment. Second, real-time market data integration through job-portal APIs will be explored to keep compensation benchmarks current. Third, the knowledge base will be expanded from 60 to over 500 contracts across diverse industries, improving RAG evidence quality. Fourth, reinforcement learning will be investigated for optimizing negotiation strategies based on historical outcome data. Finally, legal compliance checks will be extended through integration with databases covering the Shops and Establishments Act, the Industrial Disputes Act, and the Code on Wages, 2019.
