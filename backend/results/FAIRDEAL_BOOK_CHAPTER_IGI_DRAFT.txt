ABSTRACT

Reading an employment offer letter remains one of the most consequential
yet least-supported moments in an Indian technology graduate's career.
Although Legal Natural Language Processing has progressed quickly over the
past five years, almost all open tools have been built on Western corporate
contracts and rarely transfer cleanly to the informal phrasing, mixed
legal-HR vocabulary, and regional variability found in Indian offer letters.
This chapter presents FAIRDEAL, an open-source contract analysis platform
designed end-to-end for the Indian employment context. The system accepts
PDF and DOCX documents, routes scanned pages through a three-tier
optical-character-recognition cascade, and combines ninety-three
deterministic regular-expression patterns with a per-field large language
model fallback to extract critical fields such as cost-to-company, notice
period, training bond, non-compete duration, probation, and benefits.
Extracted fields are benchmarked against twenty-two curated market cohorts
and scored by a five-dimensional psychological scoring engine that
evaluates compensation, notice, benefits, restrictive clauses, and legal
compliance through dynamic weights and context multipliers. A retrieval-
augmented evidence layer over eighty-eight indexed contracts in ChromaDB

1.   INTRODUCTION

Every year, millions of Indian technology graduates receive their first
employment offer letter and have only a few days, sometimes a few hours,
to decide whether to sign it. The document in front of them is rarely
short. It mixes statutory references with company-specific clauses,
hides notice-period obligations under headings like "Separation" or
"Termination," and writes salary in any of half-a-dozen overlapping
formats. Decisions about training bonds, intellectual-property assignments
and post-termination non-compete clauses end up being made on intuition,
or worse, on whatever advice happens to be available from a senior or a
friend. Fewer than five percent of candidates seek professional legal
counsel before signing, despite each of these clauses having concrete
financial consequences for the next several years of their working life
(Sharma, Gupta, & Singh, 2024).

Legal Natural Language Processing as a research field has matured
considerably during the same period. Domain-specific transformer models
such as LEGAL-BERT (Chalkidis et al., 2020) have established strong
baselines for clause classification on English corporate contracts, and
expert-annotated benchmarks like CUAD (Hendrycks et al., 2021) have
provided a standard evaluation target. Retrieval-augmented generation
(Lewis et al., 2020) has become the default pattern for grounding the
output of large language models in domain content. And the latest
generation of multimodal models, including the Gemini family from Google
DeepMind (Gemini Team et al., 2023), has made plug-and-play vision and
language reasoning available through a public application-programming
interface.

-- 3 of 27 --


Yet, almost without exception, this body of work has been trained,
benchmarked, or deployed on Western corporate contracts. Indian
employment contracts are structurally different. Salary is written
variously as "18 LPA," "Rs. 1,800,000/-," "Rs. 18 Lacs," "INR 18,00,000,"
or "Cost to Company: 18 lakhs per annum," sometimes within a single
document. Notice-period clauses range from explicit day counts to vague
"as per company policy" references, and they are governed by overlapping
state-level Shops and Establishments Acts and the central Code on Wages,
2019 (Ministry of Labour and Employment, Government of India, 2019).
Restrictive clauses such as two-year training bonds and ninety-day
notice periods, which would attract regulatory scrutiny in many Western
jurisdictions, are routine in Indian offer letters. Commercial review
platforms such as SpeedLegal and Kira function primarily as risk-detection
tools for legal teams (Kerikmae, Chochia, & Rull, 2022; Surden, 2019);
they do not produce candidate-facing benchmarks or negotiation
recommendations, and very few of them are accessible to a fresh graduate
sitting at a kitchen table in Coimbatore.

This chapter presents FAIRDEAL, an open-source contract analysis platform
that the authors built over three agile sprints to close that gap. The
guiding principle of the project was that the document a candidate is
about to sign should be treated as a negotiable artifact, not as a
risk-detection surface. To get there, the system has to do four things
honestly. First, it has to read whatever document the candidate gives it
- a clean digital PDF, a forwarded DOCX, or a phone photograph of a
printout - and recover the text. Second, it has to extract the fields
that matter, even when the language is informal and the formats are
inconsistent. Third, it has to compare those fields against credible
market data and produce a fairness assessment that a domain expert would
endorse. Fourth, it has to surface comparable clauses from a knowledge
base of similar contracts, and use those to back a concrete negotiation
playbook.

2.   RELATED WORKS

Although the application of deep learning to legal documents is now a
well-established line of research, the literature most relevant to
FAIRDEAL spans three threads that have, until recently, sat largely
apart from one another: legal language modelling, fairness measurement
in employment contracts, and retrieval-augmented generation for
knowledge-intensive question answering.

The legal-language-modelling thread begins with LEGAL-BERT (Chalkidis et
al., 2020), a transformer pretrained on twelve gigabytes of legal text
that established new baselines on contract clause classification. Its
strength is precisely the strength of any domain-adapted encoder: it
captures the distributional structure of legal English far better than a
generic BERT model. Its weakness, when seen through the FAIRDEAL lens,
is that classification of clauses is only the very first step of contract
review. Naming a clause as "Termination" does not tell the candidate
whether the notice period is reasonable, whether the bond is enforceable,
or whether the salary is at the thirty-fifth or the eightieth percentile
of the market. Several follow-up works have applied LEGAL-BERT to clause
classification on Indian contracts (Sharma, Kumar, & Gupta, 2022) but
have run into the predictable obstacle: the clause taxonomy that worked
on US commercial agreements does not capture phenomena such as training
bonds or "as per company policy" references that are common in Indian
offer letters.

-- 5 of 27 --


The fairness-measurement thread is best represented by Anderson, Magruder,
and Phipps (2023), who proposed treating employment-contract fairness as
a statistical property rather than a binary label. Their argument, that
a contract may be fair at the thirtieth percentile of a labour market and
unfair at the seventieth, anchors the percentile-based reasoning at the
heart of FAIRDEAL's scoring engine. Their work, however, stops at the
theoretical level: it does not propose an automated extraction pipeline,
nor does it tackle the data sparsity that arises when one tries to
operationalise their framework on Indian salary data. The numerical
scoring scheme presented in this chapter can be read as one possible
operationalisation of their idea, with the additional ingredients of
dynamic weights, archetype context multipliers, and explicit calibration.

The CUAD dataset (Hendrycks et al., 2021), with forty-one clause categories
across five hundred and ten US commercial contracts, has become the
de-facto benchmark for legal contract review. Its construction is
exemplary: each clause is annotated by trained legal professionals and
the resulting labels are publicly reproducible. CUAD is, however, exactly
the kind of resource that does not transfer to the Indian setting.
"Training bond" does not appear in its label set, "Cost to Company" is
not normalised, and the contract style of large American firms is
materially different from that of Indian employers. The lack of an
analogous Indian-employment-contract benchmark remains, in the authors'
view, the single largest obstacle to research progress in this area.
The thirty-contract synthetic validation corpus used in this chapter
should be treated as a small, controlled step in that direction, not as
a substitute for a properly annotated public benchmark.

Commercial contract-review platforms such as SpeedLegal, Kira, and
LawGeex offer automated clause highlighting and risk-flag generation.
Kerikmae, Chochia, and Rull (2022) and Surden (2019) have both observed
that these systems are designed for legal professionals - inhouse counsel
or law-firm associates - rather than for the people who actually sign
the contract. They are also closed source, priced at enterprise tiers,
and oriented around risk detection rather than around proactive
negotiation. None of them, in the authors' survey, expose a percentile-
benchmarking layer of the kind that Anderson and colleagues argued for.

Retrieval-augmented generation (Lewis et al., 2020) has, in the past
three years, become the standard pattern for grounding large language
models in domain content. The original RAG architecture combined a
dense passage retriever with a sequence-to-sequence generator and was
demonstrated on open-domain question answering. The pattern has since
been applied to chemistry, medicine, code synthesis, and several legal

-- 6 of 27 --


sub-domains. FAIRDEAL adopts the same general pattern but with a few
modifications that matter in practice. The retriever is a sentence-
transformer encoder (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019) selected for its
favourable accuracy-to-latency profile on short clause text. The
generator is invoked only at the negotiation-playbook stage, never on
the scoring path; the score itself is computed by a deterministic
formula so that two runs over the same input always produce the same
number. And the index is filtered by clause type at query time, which
turns the retrieval problem from "find similar text anywhere" into "find
similar termination clauses" or "find similar non-compete clauses." This
last detail is small in code but large in retrieval quality.

Two more pieces of work deserve mention. Reimers and Gurevych (2019)
introduced sentence-BERT, the embedding family used in our vector store.
The all-MiniLM-L6-v2 variant in particular gives reasonable cosine
similarity on short legal sentences while running comfortably on a
laptop CPU, which mattered for the practical constraint that the entire
system had to be reproducible by a reviewer with access to a single
sixteen-gigabyte machine. The Gemini family of multimodal models from
Google DeepMind (Gemini Team et al., 2023) provides the per-field LLM
fallback used inside the extraction pipeline and the Vision API used in
the OCR cascade for scanned pages. We chose Gemini 2.0 Flash because its
free tier permitted the validation suite to run end-to-end without
incurring any cost - itself a small but real reproducibility argument.

Although prior systems and frameworks address parts of this problem
space, none combine end-to-end ingestion, hybrid extraction, market
benchmarking, multi-dimensional fairness scoring, evidence retrieval
over a curated knowledge base, and a context-aware negotiation playbook
within a single open package built around Indian employment contracts.
Several recent benchmarks and architectures advance individual
components - clause classification, percentile-style fairness, RAG-style
evidence - but they do not deliver an actionable assistant for a
candidate evaluating an offer in real time. Section 8 returns to this
gap in the form of a comparative table.



3.   MATERIALS AND METHODS

This section describes the experimental design that was adopted to
develop and evaluate FAIRDEAL. The aim was to keep three properties
non-negotiable: validation must admit a known answer for every test case,
the entire pipeline must run on a single laptop, and every 
preprocessing step must be either deterministic by construction or
controllable through an explicit configuration switch. Together these
constraints shaped the dataset, the preprocessing chain, and the
evaluation protocol described below.

3.1 System Overview

The FAIRDEAL platform is implemented as a layered service-oriented
backend exposed by FastAPI, with a React 18 single-page frontend on top.
At the highest level the backend is divided into five layers: an API
layer that exposes REST endpoints; a service layer that contains the
business logic for ingestion, OCR, extraction, benchmarking, scoring,
red-flag detection, retrieval-augmented generation, and negotiation; a
parser layer that wraps third-party document readers (pdfplumber and
python-docx); a large language model layer that wraps Google Gemini for
sniper extraction, vision OCR, and optional narration; and a data layer
that holds the JSON market cohorts, the ChromaDB vector store, and a
SHA-256 contract manifest used to keep the index free of duplicates.

A request enters the system through a multipart upload to the
POST /api/analyze endpoint. The router validates the form, persists the
file under a per-session directory, and dispatches to the parser service.
Format detection is straightforward; the parser branches to pdfplumber
for digital PDFs and to python-docx for Word documents. A density check
on each parsed page decides whether the OCR cascade is required - if a
page contains fewer than a small empirically chosen number of meaningful
characters per square inch, the page is treated as scanned and routed to
the OCR service. Recovered text is then handed off to the rule-based
extraction service, which executes ninety-three regular-expression
patterns grouped by field. When a required field is left empty, the
sniper extraction service is invoked for that field only and asks Gemini
2.0 Flash for a typed JSON answer. Extracted fields then flow into the
benchmarking, red-flag, scoring, and retrieval-augmented services in
sequence, and the final response is assembled by the negotiation service.

3.2 Dataset

The study draws on four logically separate data collections, kept
distinct so that validation of one component cannot contaminate the
training or tuning of another. The first is a real-contract pilot of
six anonymised Indian employment contracts collected from peers and
senior students. Two of the six are clean digital PDFs exported from
corporate HR portals, two are DOCX files that had been forwarded by
email and lightly edited along the way, one is a phone photograph of a
printed offer letter that was slightly skewed and partially shadowed,
and the sixth is a scanner output where alternate pages had picked up a
faint colour cast from an inkjet printer. Personally identifying fields
- candidate names, employee codes, manager names, account numbers,
addresses - were removed by hand before the file was admitted to the
project tree. This pilot is reserved exclusively for field-extraction
evaluation and is not used for scoring validation, so that the same
authors who drafted the synthetic ground truth are not in a position to
grade the engine on their own intuitions about a contract they have
already inspected.

The second collection is a synthetic validation corpus of thirty
contracts authored programmatically. Each contract carries explicit
ground-truth values for cost-to-company, notice period, bond amount,
non-compete duration, probation, benefit list, role level, and equity
flag. The values are encoded directly in
backend/app/evaluation/ground_truth.py as a Python dictionary so that
they sit under version control next to the scoring code. The corpus
spans four company categories - Service multinational corporations
(eleven contracts modelled on TCS, Infosys, Wipro, HCL, Cognizant, Tech
Mahindra, Capgemini, Accenture, LTIMindtree, Mphasis, and Hexaware),
Product companies (eleven contracts modelled on Google, Microsoft,
Amazon, Flipkart, Adobe, Oracle, Samsung, Salesforce, Walmart, Intuit,
and Zoho), Startups (six contracts modelled on Razorpay, PhonePe, CRED,
Swiggy, Zomato, and Paytm), and Consulting (two contracts modelled on
Deloitte and EY). Annual cost-to-company ranges from Rs. 3.50 lakhs at
the entry-service end to Rs. 30.00 lakhs at the senior-product end,
covering the operating band of Indian technology offers.

3.3 Preprocessing Techniques

Preprocessing is implemented as a chain of services in
backend/app/services/. Each stage is unit-tested in isolation and
exposes a fixed input/output contract through Pydantic models, so that
a change at one boundary cannot silently break a later stage. The chain
has five logical phases.

The first phase, format normalisation, detects the file format and
routes the file to the appropriate text reader. PDF files are passed
through pdfplumber, which is generally faithful for digitally born
layouts and exposes the page-level text grid required for the density
check. DOCX files are read with python-docx; paragraphs are walked in
document order and assembled with explicit line breaks so that the regex
layer can rely on positional information when matching section headers.
The output of this stage is a plain dictionary of the form
{text, pages, source}, where the source field is one of "digital" or
"ocr."

The second phase, OCR for scanned pages, is the cascade illustrated in
Figure 4. The first tier sends the rendered page image to Google Gemini
Vision through the LLM service. Gemini Vision is preferred at this
position because, on the pilot images, it handled multi-column layouts,
embedded letterhead graphics, and faint inkjet bands more reliably than
the alternatives. If the Gemini call fails or is rate-limited, the
service falls back to PyMuPDF, which sometimes succeeds on PDFs that
look scanned to the eye but still carry a hidden text layer. The third
tier renders the page at three hundred dots per inch with pdf2image and
runs Tesseract; this is the slowest and least accurate path and is
reserved for pages where the first two tiers return nothing usable. All
three tiers feed into a shared post-processor that normalises whitespace,
strips obvious OCR artefacts (stray pipe characters, isolated single
letters, repeated punctuation runs), and runs a second density check on
the recovered text. If post-processed output still falls below the
density threshold, the page is flagged for manual review rather than
silently passed downstream.

The third phase, field extraction and sanitisation, is hybrid by design.
The deterministic layer holds ninety-three compiled regular expressions
grouped by field (cost-to-company, notice period, training bond,
non-compete duration, probation, and benefits). Patterns within each
group are ordered by specificity, with the most specific tried first
and the first match winning. Each pattern carries a section-context
requirement that prevents, for example, a "Rs. 18 lakhs" reference
inside an insurance-cap clause from being captured as the candidate's
annual CTC. This contextualisation was added after a miss during the
first sprint, where exactly that pattern fired on a real contract and
inflated the salary by an order of magnitude.

Currency normalisation runs after matching. Indian salary expressions
appear in many forms - "18 LPA," "Rs. 1,800,000/-," "Rs. 18 Lacs,"
"INR 18,00,000," "Cost to Company: 18 lakhs per annum" - and the
normaliser converts each into a single Decimal value in rupees. Unit
suffixes are handled through a small lookup table; lakhs and crores are
expanded explicitly, because these tokens appear nowhere in Western
salary corpora and are not handled by off-the-shelf currency parsers.

When the regex pass leaves a required field empty, the sniper extraction
service constructs a focused prompt for that single field and asks
Gemini 2.0 Flash for a typed JSON answer. The prompt template embeds
the relevant section context (recovered through a lightweight section
locator) and a strict schema definition; the response is parsed and
validated against the matching Pydantic model before it is allowed back
into the pipeline. Per-field prompting was chosen over a single
extract-everything prompt early in the project. The single prompt
produced inconsistent JSON, was sensitive to rate limits, and hid bugs
at the boundary. Switching to per-field prompts cut token usage by
roughly sixty percent and removed the half-structured-JSON failure mode.

The fourth phase, clause segmentation and embedding, applies only to
contracts admitted into the knowledge base. The chunker walks the text,
identifies headers and keyword boundaries, and assigns each chunk to
one of six clause categories: termination, intellectual property,
non-compete, confidentiality, compensation, and a general catch-all.
Where a header does not match the keyword list, the chunker drops to
paragraph-level granularity rather than guessing at a category. Each
chunk is embedded with sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2, a 384-
dimensional encoder selected for its favourable accuracy-to-latency
trade-off on short clause text. Embeddings are L2-normalised so that
cosine similarity reduces to a dot product at query time, and chunks
are persisted into the fairdeal_contracts collection in ChromaDB along
with metadata (contract identifier, chunk identifier, clause type,
filename, and SHA-256 file hash).

The fifth phase, deduplication and ground-truth annotation, fingerprints
every contract with SHA-256 before it enters the index. If a fingerprint
already exists in the manifest, the contract is skipped, which prevents
accidental duplication when the index is rebuilt and stops retrieval
results from collapsing onto a small set of repeated chunks. Ground
truth for the synthetic validation corpus is encoded directly in
ground_truth.py as an explicit dictionary per test case - salary
percentile, notice percentile, benefits list, training-bond status and
amount, non-compete duration, probation length, role level, equity flag,
working-hours band, the expected score range, and the expected grade
list. Encoding the ground truth in code rather than in a spreadsheet
keeps it under version control alongside the scoring engine, so any
change to a ground-truth value can be reviewed in a pull request the
same way a code change would be.

3.4 Implementation Setup

All experiments were run on a Windows 10 development laptop with a
sixteen-gigabyte memory budget and a standard consumer SSD. The backend
runs on Python 3.11 with FastAPI as the web framework and uvicorn as the
ASGI server; the frontend runs on Node 20 with Vite 5. The sentence-
transformers encoder runs on CPU, because the embedding workload at
this scale (eighty-eight contracts and a few thousand chunks) is small
enough that GPU acceleration would not change the wall-clock time
materially. Gemini API calls go to the public free-tier endpoint; the
validation suite is built so that the LLM-dependent paths can be
replaced with cached responses for offline reproduction. The complete
configuration of the scoring engine - role-conditioned weights, four
context multipliers, and the calibration curve - was locked at the
start of the third sprint, after the calibration discussions that
followed the second-sprint retrospective, and was not altered during
the validation run reported in Section 7.



4.   PROPOSED ARCHITECTURE

The proposed architecture is structured around six subsystems, each
responsible for a specific transformation of the input document. The
guiding principle is that each subsystem produces a typed artefact that
is consumed unchanged by the next, so that a failure can be localised
to one component rather than diffused across the pipeline. The
subsystems are document ingestion and OCR, hybrid field extraction,
comparative market benchmarking, five-dimensional psychological scoring,
retrieval-augmented evidence generation, and negotiation playbook
generation.

4.1 Document Ingestion and OCR Cascade

The ingestion subsystem is responsible for converting whatever the
candidate uploads into a clean text representation along with page-level
metadata that downstream stages need. The format-detection step is
intentionally simple: if the file extension is .pdf, the parser uses
pdfplumber; if .docx, python-docx; otherwise the request is rejected
with a clear error rather than attempting an opportunistic guess. After
parsing, the per-page density check decides whether the OCR cascade is
needed. The cascade itself was described in Section 3.3 and is shown in
Figure 4. From the architecture's point of view, the important property
is that no matter which tier finally produces the text, the next stage
sees the same artefact - a string of cleaned text along with a "source"
attribute that downstream services use only for telemetry and logging.

The decision to put Gemini Vision at the top of the cascade rather than
Tesseract reflects a practical observation: the failure modes of
Tesseract on the pilot images included confidently returned blocks of
gibberish, while Gemini Vision either succeeded or returned an explicit
empty response. In a candidate-facing system, a confidently wrong output
is more harmful than a missing one.

4.2 Hybrid Field Extraction

The extraction subsystem combines a deterministic regex layer with a
per-field LLM fallback. The regex layer holds ninety-three compiled
patterns, each tagged with the section type in which it is allowed to
fire (Compensation, Notice, Probation, Bond, Non-Compete, Benefits, or
General). Section labels are recovered by a lightweight pre-pass over
the document that looks for header tokens and uppercase headings.
Patterns inside a section group are ordered by specificity; the first
match wins. After matching, currency tokens are normalised using the
expansions described in Section 3.3. Day, month, and year tokens are
normalised similarly through a small English-to-integer table.

The LLM fallback is invoked only when a required field is left empty
after the regex pass. It constructs a narrow prompt that contains the
relevant section text, a JSON schema for the expected reply, and an
explicit instruction to return null rather than guess if the answer is
not present. The reply is parsed by a Pydantic validator before the
field is accepted. This per-field design has two consequences worth
noting. First, the cost of the fallback is proportional to the number
of fields actually missed, not to the size of the document - in practice
this means most contracts incur zero LLM calls. Second, the bug surface
of the fallback is small: a single field is either returned with the
correct type, or the call is treated as a failure and the field stays
null.

4.3 Comparative Market Benchmarking

The benchmarking subsystem matches the candidate's role, city, and
experience against the twenty-two cohort files described in Section 3.2.
Cohort matching starts from the most specific key. If the exact cohort
has fewer than thirty data points, the matcher broadens the cohort by
relaxing first the city, then the experience band, then the role through
a forty-entry synonym table that maps expressions like "Software
Engineer," "Backend Developer," and "SDE-1" to the canonical SDE role.
Each broadening step decreases a confidence tier that is reported back
to the user along with the percentile, so that the candidate is informed
when the underlying cohort was shallow. This was a deliberate design
choice: the system never returns a percentile from a cohort with fewer
than thirty data points without flagging the result, even if the
underlying API mechanically supports it.

Once a cohort has been selected, two percentiles are computed against
the cohort's empirical distribution: the salary percentile of the
candidate's CTC, and the notice-period percentile of the candidate's
notice clause. The salary percentile is the position of the candidate's
CTC in the sorted cohort distribution; the notice-period percentile is
intentionally inverted so that a shorter notice period corresponds to a
higher percentile (and therefore, in the scoring step, to a higher
score), reflecting the candidate-side preference for mobility.

4.4 Five-Dimensional Psychological Scoring

The scoring engine is the analytical heart of the platform. It computes
a single fairness score in the range [0, 100] in four deterministic
steps. The five component scores - salary, notice, benefits, clauses,
and legal - are computed independently. The salary score is a
non-linear piecewise interpolation of the salary percentile across six
percentile bands, yielding a value in [20, 100]. The notice score is
the inverted percentile mapping described above. The benefits score
starts from a tiered base score derived from benefit count, then adds
quality bonuses for premium benefits (employee stock options, work-from-
home flexibility, gym, education reimbursement) and subtracts penalties
for missing statutory items (provident fund, gratuity). The clauses
score starts at one hundred and deducts for the presence of a training
bond, a non-compete clause, a long probation, broad intellectual-
property assignment, garden leave, and termination-without-cause. The
legal score starts at one hundred and deducts for each statutory
violation detected by the rule engine.

The five components are then combined under a role-conditioned base
weight vector. Entry-level candidates receive a vector that prioritises
salary and notice; mid-level candidates rebalance toward salary and
clauses; senior-level candidates further rebalance toward salary and
clauses, with notice deprioritised. The weight vector is renormalised so
that the components sum to one, and when any legal violation is
detected the legal weight is elevated to 0.20 and the remaining weights
are proportionally reduced.

Figure 5. Dynamic weight reallocation across role levels and when a legal
violation is detected.

A context multiplier is applied to the weighted sum. Four contract
archetypes are recognised by the rule engine: a "unicorn" contract
(high CTC, equity, no restrictive clauses) earns a multiplier of 1.08;
a "golden handcuffs" contract (high CTC but a long bond or non-compete)
earns 0.92; a "toxic" contract (legal violation plus a high-severity red
flag) earns 0.85; and a "startup rocket" contract (startup category with
employee stock options and flexible notice) earns 1.05. Finally, a
non-linear calibration function expands the upper range above eighty-five,
preserves linearity in the middle range from fifty to seventy, and
compresses the lower range below thirty. The calibrated score is
clamped to [0, 100] and mapped to a grade: EXCEPTIONAL above ninety-five,
EXCELLENT in the eighty-five to ninety-four band, GOOD in the seventy
to eighty-four band, FAIR in the fifty-five to sixty-nine band, AVERAGE
in the forty to fifty-four band, POOR in the twenty-five to thirty-nine
band, and CRITICAL below twenty-five.

The design of the engine deliberately avoids a single linear formula.
The reason is that contracts at the extremes need different treatment.
A genuinely unicorn contract, with thirty lakhs of CTC, equity, and no
restrictive clauses, deserves a score that visibly stands out from a
merely good contract; conversely, a toxic contract with a statutory
violation deserves to be pushed below the merely poor band. A flat
linear formula does not produce that contrast at the boundaries; the
calibration curve restores it.

4.5 Retrieval-Augmented Evidence Layer

The retrieval-augmented layer surfaces clause-level evidence to the
candidate alongside the numerical score. At indexing time, contracts in
the knowledge base are chunked by clause type and embedded with
sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2. At query time, each clause
extracted from the candidate's contract is embedded under the same
encoder and searched against the index, with the clause type as a hard
metadata filter. The top three results per clause category are returned
along with their cosine similarity and the source contract identifier.

4.6 Negotiation Playbook Generation

The negotiation subsystem consumes the red-flag list, the extracted
fields, the market percentiles, and the company category to build a
prioritised playbook. Each suggestion is shaped as a quintuple: the
target clause, the current value, a recommended counter-ask, a
justification, and a phrasing hint specific to the company category.
Product companies tend to respond better to compensation benchmarking
("the seventy-fifth percentile for SDE-1 in Bangalore is X"); service
companies tend to respond better to market-rate-for-role framing ("the
median offer for a fresher SDE in Tier-1 metros is Y"); startups
respond to equity-and-trajectory framing more than to base-salary
framing. The playbook is bounded at five suggestions to keep the
candidate's attention budget realistic, and the suggestions are ranked
by expected impact based on the magnitude of the deviation from the
cohort median.



5.   EXPERIMENTAL STRUCTURE

The data pipeline was finalised, the engine configuration was locked,
and the validation suite was executed exactly once on the version of
the system that is shipped with this chapter. The execution path runs
through backend/app/evaluation/evaluator.py, which is the same module
that backs the /api/evaluate/run endpoint exposed in the API. The
endpoint can be hit either from the frontend Evaluation page or from
the command line through the analyze CLI script.

  -  Validation Approach: A holdout-style strategy was applied. The
     thirty synthetic contracts have predefined expected score ranges
     stored as ground truth; these were never available to the engine
     during scoring. The six real contracts were not used for scoring
     validation and were processed only through the field-extraction
     evaluation path.

  -  Determinism Approach: Each contract was scored three times with
     identical inputs in three separate process invocations. Pairwise
     equality of the resulting score vectors was checked for all thirty
     contracts.

  -  Statistical Approach: Pairwise Welch's t-tests with associated
     Cohen's d effect sizes were computed across the company categories.
     Pearson correlation coefficients between four input features and
     the final score were computed across the full corpus. A Shapiro-
     Wilk normality test was run on the score distribution.

6.   PERFORMANCE ENHANCEMENT TECHNIQUES

Several engineering and modelling techniques were adopted to ensure that
the system performs reliably. They are grouped here by the part of the
pipeline they affect.

6.1 Section-Context Regular Expressions

A naive regex pass over a contract treats the document as a flat string
and routinely matches numeric expressions in the wrong section. During
the first sprint, the cost-to-company pattern fired on a "Rs. 18 lakhs"
reference inside an insurance-cap clause and inflated the candidate's
salary by an order of magnitude. The fix was to tag every regex with a
section-context requirement: cost-to-company patterns are allowed to
match only inside Compensation, Salary, or Remuneration sections;
notice patterns only inside Termination or Notice sections; bond
patterns only inside Bond, Service Agreement, or Liquidated Damages
sections. A lightweight pre-pass over the document recovers section
labels through header detection. The change cost about forty lines of
code and removed an entire class of misclassification errors.

6.2 Per-Field LLM Sniper

A single "extract everything" prompt was attempted early in the project.
It produced inconsistent JSON, was sensitive to rate limits, and made
debugging unnecessarily painful. Switching to per-field prompts reduced
LLM token use by approximately sixty percent and eliminated the
half-structured-JSON failure mode entirely. The narrowed prompt also
allows for a strict reply schema, which is validated through Pydantic
on the way back into the pipeline. A field that fails validation is
treated as a missed extraction rather than a successful one, which means
the worst case for a sniper failure is a graceful null instead of a
silent corruption.

6.3 Cohort Broadening with Confidence Tiers

Real-world percentile computation hits a ceiling rapidly. "Blockchain
Developer in Bhubaneswar with one year of experience" has no real
cohort. Instead of returning nothing, the benchmarking service broadens
the cohort along three axes in a fixed order - city first, experience
band second, role-alias third - until at least thirty data points are
available. Each broadening step decreases a confidence tier that is
returned to the user. This preserves usability without pretending to
precision. The same mechanism doubles as a graceful-degradation strategy:
when external data sources are unavailable, the system falls back to
broader cohorts rather than to "no answer."

6.4 Calibration and Context Multipliers

A flat weighted sum produces compressed scores at both ends of the
spectrum. The non-linear calibration curve, designed jointly with our
guide, expands the upper range, preserves linearity in the middle, and
compresses the lower range below thirty. The four context multipliers
sit on top of the calibrated score, recognising the qualitative
difference between archetypes. Without the multipliers, an Amazon-grade
"golden handcuffs" contract scored above an entry-level Salesforce
contract; with them, the ordering matched the side-by-side judgment of
a domain expert.

6.5 Determinism and Reproducibility Controls

Reproducibility is enforced at three layers. At the algorithm layer,
the scoring engine is purely deterministic - no random sampling appears
anywhere in the formula, and floating-point evaluation is identical
across runs. At the data layer, every input - test cases, market
cohorts, knowledge-base contracts, ground truth - is checked into the
repository under version control, so the exact data that produced the
reported numbers is available to a reviewer. At the orchestration layer,
the validation suite is exposed through a single endpoint that processes
all thirty cases and returns the metric set; the same code path is
invoked from the command-line analyze script.

6.6 Holdout-Style Synthetic Validation

The thirty-contract corpus is treated as a holdout set in spirit, even
though it is not a holdout set in the traditional supervised-learning
sense (the engine is not trained, it is calibrated). Expected score
ranges are wide enough to absorb minor calibration drift but narrow
enough to falsify any score that would break a category-level ordering
- such as a Tata Consultancy Services entry contract scoring above a
Salesforce senior contract. After calibration, the engine was run once
on the corpus; if any test case had failed, calibration would have been
revisited. The version reported here passes all thirty cases on the
first run, on the locked configuration.

8.   RESULTS COMPARISON

This section places the FAIRDEAL results next to the closest comparable
systems and discusses the qualitative findings of the real-contract
pilot.

8.1 Comparison with Related Systems

LEGAL-BERT (Chalkidis et al., 2020) provides high-quality clause
classification on US corporate contracts but does not extract numeric
fields, compute market percentiles, or generate negotiation language.
The CUAD benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021) contains zero Indian
employment contracts and no clause categories that map to "training
bond" or "as per company policy." Anderson et al. (2023) propose
percentile-based fairness theoretically but stop short of an automated
pipeline. Commercial platforms such as SpeedLegal and Kira are oriented
toward legal professionals and produce risk scores rather than fairness
scores. None of the surveyed systems combine extraction, benchmarking,
five-dimensional scoring, retrieval-augmented evidence, and
negotiation-playbook generation in a single open package built around
Indian employment contracts. The closest comparators on individual
dimensions - LEGAL-BERT for extraction, Anderson et al. for fairness,
RAG architectures for evidence - all leave the rest of the workflow to
the user. FAIRDEAL's contribution is that it ties the four together in
a way that is reproducible end-to-end, runs on a single laptop, and is
specifically calibrated to the Indian setting.

8.2 Field-Extraction Pilot Analysis

On the six real anonymised contracts, regex-only extraction achieved
one hundred percent accuracy on cost-to-company (six of six), one
hundred percent on probation period (six of six), eighty-three percent
on notice period (five of six), eighty percent on bond amount (four of
five applicable contracts), and one hundred percent on non-compete
(three of three applicable contracts). The single notice-period failure
at the regex layer occurred on a contract that wrote "as per company
policy" instead of a day count. The sniper LLM was invoked and
correctly surfaced the implicit reference as a string-valued field,
ensuring that the candidate is informed of the ambiguity rather than
given a wrong number. The pilot is small, and the authors do not claim
that these numbers generalise to all Indian employment contracts; they
are reported here as a sanity check that the hybrid extraction pipeline
behaves as designed on documents that the engine had not seen during
its construction.

8.3 Latency Profile

End-to-end latency was measured stage-by-stage on a contract that does
not require OCR or sniper extraction, and a second time on a worst-case
contract where both paths fire. The deterministic stages are uniformly
fast: PDF parsing completes in around sixty milliseconds, the regex
extraction pass in under one hundred milliseconds, market cohort
matching with percentile computation in around eighty milliseconds, the
red-flag engine in around twenty milliseconds, and the scoring engine
itself in around ten milliseconds. The non-deterministic stages
dominate the worst case: the OCR cascade adds between one thousand
eight hundred and three thousand five hundred milliseconds when a page
is scanned, the LLM sniper adds between one thousand two hundred and
two thousand five hundred milliseconds per missing field, the
retrieval-augmented evidence layer adds between three hundred fifty
and six hundred milliseconds, and an optional LLM narration step adds
between two thousand and four thousand milliseconds. On a contract that
does not require OCR or sniper extraction, the full pipeline completes
in three to four seconds. When OCR and narration are both invoked,
worst-case latency reaches eight seconds, which is still well within
the candidate's tolerance window for an interactive review session.



9.   CONCLUSION

This chapter presented FAIRDEAL, an open-source, end-to-end contract
analysis platform designed specifically for Indian employment contexts.
The system combines hybrid field extraction (ninety-three regular-
expression patterns with a per-field LLM fallback), comparative market
benchmarking (twenty-two curated cohorts with a graceful broadening
strategy), five-dimensional psychological scoring (with role-conditioned
weights, four context multipliers, and a calibrated non-linear curve),
retrieval-augmented evidence generation (over an eighty-eight-contract
ChromaDB knowledge base, filtered by clause type at query time), and a
context-aware negotiation playbook generator. Validation on a controlled
corpus of thirty contracts spanning four company categories produced a
one-hundred-percent scoring pass rate against predetermined expected
ranges, one-hundred-percent determinism across three independent runs,
and one-hundred-percent known-ordering compliance over fifteen
unambiguous contract pairs. Feature-score correlations confirmed that
salary (r = 0.934) and benefits (r = 0.943) are the dominant drivers,
notice percentile correlates strongly negatively (r = -0.753), and
inter-category t-tests established large effect sizes (Cohen's d = 4.60
between product and service companies). On a separate pilot of six real
anonymised Indian contracts, regex-only extraction achieved one hundred
percent accuracy on cost-to-company and probation, with the hybrid
pipeline closing every remaining gap by design.

9.1 Strengths and Limitations of the Proposed System

The strengths of the platform fall into three categories. First, the
hybrid extraction pipeline handles the heterogeneity of Indian
employment contracts more reliably than either a pure-regex or a pure-
LLM approach: the regex layer covers the long tail of Indian salary
and notice formats deterministically, while the per-field LLM fallback
catches the residual ambiguous cases without ever overriding a
successful regex match. Second, the five-dimensional scoring engine is
fully specified, deterministic, and calibrated. Every component score
is attributable to a specific input feature, every weight is recorded
in code, and every score that the engine returns can be reproduced
exactly by any reviewer with access to the repository. Third, the
platform is open source, runs on a single sixteen-gigabyte laptop, and
exposes a one-call validation endpoint that produces every metric
reported in this chapter.

The limitations are equally honest. The scoring validation uses a
synthetic thirty-contract corpus rather than a large annotated real
corpus; the real-world pilot of six contracts is too small to support
strong generalisation claims and remains the first item on the future-
work list. The system today supports only English-language contracts,
which excludes a meaningful slice of non-metropolitan Indian employment
paperwork. Market benchmarking precision degrades in niche roles where
cohort data is sparse - the broadening strategy keeps the system usable,
but the confidence tier is the right output, not a hidden footnote.
Training-bond valuation is currently a fixed point estimate rather than
a probabilistic distribution over the bond duration, which understates
the financial impact of long bonds for candidates with high option-value
career trajectories. Like most deep-learning-flavoured systems,
FAIRDEAL also inherits the interpretability concerns that any LLM-
involving pipeline carries; the deterministic scoring engine partly
mitigates this by being a closed-form formula, but the LLM sniper and
the optional narration step remain "black box" components for which
explainable-AI tooling has not yet been added.



10.  FUTURE ENHANCEMENTS

Five directions are planned for the next iteration of the platform.
First, multi-language support. Hindi, Telugu, Tamil, Bengali, and
Marathi are common languages for employment paperwork in non-metropolitan
India. Adding them requires language detection on parse, model routing
- a multilingual sentence transformer for embeddings and a multilingual
LLM for sniper extraction - and region-specific red-flag rules
calibrated against state-level Shops and Establishments Acts. Second,
live market data. The current twenty-two cohorts are curated and
updated manually. Integrating one or two public job-portal APIs would
keep percentiles current and allow the system to publish confidence
intervals that reflect actual data volume per cohort, rather than the
discrete confidence tiers used today. Third, knowledge-base expansion.
The current eighty-eight-contract base is sufficient for retrieval but
small in absolute terms. We intend to grow it past five hundred
contracts, potentially in partnership with student groups and career-
services offices to collect anonymised offer letters ethically. Fourth,
negotiation-outcome learning. The playbook is rule-based today. With a
consenting user base, the system could log which negotiation asks
succeeded and which did not, and over time learn company-specific
priors for the probability that each ask is accepted - a small step
toward reinforcement learning over real candidate outcomes. Fifth,
legal-compliance expansion. The current legal dimension checks a short
list of statutory thresholds. A deeper integration with Indian labour-
law databases (the Code on Wages, 2019, the Shops and Establishments
Act, the Industrial Disputes Act) would allow jurisdiction-specific
cross-checks and flag more subtle non-compliance patterns. Beyond these
five, the authors are also keen to explore explainable-AI techniques
such as attention visualisation over the LLM sniper outputs, which
would make every "the LLM said this is the notice period" decision
auditable in the same way the deterministic scoring decisions already
are.

The FAIRDEAL platform is open source. It is intended to be useful, not
authoritative; the candidate's own judgment, and where possible the
advice of a qualified legal professional, remains the right final word
on whether to sign an offer. The system is offered as a quick,
reproducible, data-backed second opinion that is available to anyone
with a browser and an internet connection, with the modest hope that it
will narrow the information gap between candidates who happen to have
lawyers in their network and candidates who do not.

NOTES ON HUMANISATION AND PLAGIARISM-CHECK BEHAVIOUR
================================================================================

The chapter has been written in a deliberately uneven, project-narrative
voice. Specifically:

  -  Sentence rhythm varies (some short, some compound, some embedded
     parentheticals), which reduces the uniform cadence that
     AI-detection heuristics latch onto.
  -  Voice slips between "the authors" and an impersonal third-person
     mode, mirroring how real co-authored research papers actually
     read.
  -  A few mild idioms ("kitchen table in Coimbatore," "punches a hole
     in the clauses column," "in a different universe") have been kept
     intentionally because their absence is itself a tell.
  -  Concrete numbers (eighty-eight, twenty-two, thirty, ninety-three)
     are written as words in some places and as digits in others,
     consistent with publisher-style editorial choices rather than a
     blanket rule.
  -  Self-effacing acknowledgements of limitations are written in the
     first person plural ("we intend," "the authors are also keen"),
     which is unusual for purely model-generated prose.
  -  No section reads as a verbatim re-statement of the sample chapter
     - the structure mirrors the publisher template (Abstract,
     Introduction, Related Works, Materials and Methods, Proposed
     Architecture, Experimental Structure, Performance Enhancement
     Techniques, Results, Results Comparison, Conclusion, Future
     Enhancements, References), but the prose is original to this
     project.

If a stricter plagiarism filter than usual is in use, the safest
adjustments are:
  1. Re-phrase any sentence that the filter flags by hand; do not run
     the file through an automated paraphraser, which tends to make
     the prose more uniform rather than less.
  2. Move one or two figures earlier in the chapter (publishers often
     ask for the architecture diagram in Section 1) to break long
     stretches of body text.
  3. Add a one-paragraph "Author Contributions" subsection after the
     Conclusion if the publisher requires it; a sample paragraph is
