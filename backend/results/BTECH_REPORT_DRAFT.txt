ABSTRACT
================================================================================

Employment contract analysis continues to be a difficult problem for Indian
job seekers. Offer letters appear as well-structured PDFs, as scanned images
produced by office scanners, and as lightly-formatted DOCX files that mix
legal language with HR phrasing. Tools developed for Western corporate law
rarely generalise to this setting, and commercial options treat the document
as a risk-detection surface rather than as a negotiable artifact.

This work presents FAIRDEAL, an open-source contract analysis platform built
for the Indian employment context. The system accepts PDF and DOCX documents
and routes scanned pages through a cascaded OCR pipeline that progresses
through Google Gemini Vision, PyMuPDF, and Tesseract, so that degraded
documents are still parsed end to end. A hybrid extraction engine pairs 93
deterministic regular-expression patterns with an LLM-based sniper fallback,
allowing diverse Indian salary formats ("18 LPA", "Rs. 1,800,000/-",
"Cost to Company: 18 lakhs per annum") and implicit notice-period references
to be captured. Extracted fields are benchmarked against 22 curated market
cohorts and scored by a five-dimensional psychological scoring engine that
evaluates salary, notice period, benefits, restrictive clauses, and legal
compliance using dynamic weights and context multipliers. A retrieval-
augmented generation layer over 88 indexed contracts in ChromaDB surfaces
comparable clauses, and a context-aware negotiation playbook proposes
concrete talking points.

The project was delivered across three agile sprints, each closed with a
retrospective and review. A systematic validation on 30 contracts spanning
four company categories yielded a 100 % scoring pass rate, 100 % determinism
across three independent runs, and 100 % known-ordering compliance across
15 unambiguous contract pairs. Feature–score analysis confirmed strong
alignment between salary percentile (r = 0.934) and benefits count
(r = 0.943) with final scores, and Welch's t-tests established large
inter-category effect sizes (Cohen's d = 4.60 between product and service
companies). On six real anonymised Indian contracts, regex-only extraction
achieved 100 % accuracy for salary and probation, with the hybrid pipeline
closing every remaining gap by design.

Keywords: Contract Analysis, Retrieval-Augmented Generation, Legal NLP,
Fairness Scoring, Indian Employment Law, OCR, Hybrid Extraction.

CHAPTER 1 — INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

1.1  Introduction to Project

Every year the Indian technology industry sends out a very large number of
employment offers. A significant portion of them reach candidates who are
reading their first legal contract of any kind. The language is dense,
mixes statutory references with company-specific clauses, and is usually
accompanied by a short window for acceptance. Very few candidates consult
a lawyer before signing; most rely on friends, seniors, or a quick internet
search to decide whether the offer is reasonable. The result is an
information gap that affects everyday career decisions and, over time,
shapes how people move between jobs in the country.

Existing legal artificial-intelligence systems have largely focused on
Western corporate law. Document classification, clause tagging, and
risk detection have all been demonstrated on American datasets such as
CUAD, but the specific combination of characteristics that appears in
Indian offer letters — informal language, hybrid legal-HR terminology,
regional variation in standard practice, and highly diverse salary
expressions — is under-served by the literature. The tools that do
exist are closed source, oriented toward legal professionals, and treat
the document as something to flag rather than something to negotiate.

FAIRDEAL is our attempt to close this gap for a specific audience:
candidates who hold an offer letter in their hands and want to know
whether to accept it, re-negotiate it, or walk away. The platform accepts
PDF and DOCX files, processes scanned documents through a three-tier OCR
cascade, and extracts the fields that matter — CTC, notice period, bond
amount, non-compete duration, probation length, and the list of benefits.
It then compares these fields against 22 curated market cohorts, scores
the contract on five dimensions using a dynamic-weighting engine,
retrieves comparable clauses from a knowledge base of 88 indexed
contracts, and produces a negotiation playbook with specific talking
points. The complete pipeline runs in three to eight seconds per
contract and ships as a FastAPI backend with a React dashboard.

1.2  Problem Statement

Given an Indian employment offer letter in PDF or DOCX form, with or
without scanned pages, build a system that:

    (a) extracts the critical contract fields reliably across the range
        of formats used by Indian employers;
    (b) assesses the fairness of the contract against market benchmarks
        and Indian labour-law expectations;
    (c) retrieves evidence from comparable historical contracts to
        support the assessment; and
    (d) produces a ranked, human-readable set of negotiation
        recommendations within a few seconds of upload.

The system must remain usable when external services (LLM APIs,
cloud OCR) are temporarily unavailable, must never fail silently on
ambiguous fields, and must be reproducible enough that the same input
always yields the same score.


1.3  Motivation

Three observations drove this project.

First, contract negotiation in India is still treated as an optional,
almost cultural, activity. Candidates at the junior end of the market
rarely push back on notice-period clauses because they do not know
what the market looks like. A data-driven second opinion is, in our
experience talking to peers, the single piece of information most of
them wish they had before signing.

Second, the Large Language Model stack has matured enough that a small
team can ship a working contract analyzer without training a model from
scratch. Gemini 2.0 Flash, sentence-transformers, ChromaDB, and FastAPI
can be assembled into a system that is accurate on Indian text, cheap
to run, and respectful of rate limits. The engineering challenge is no
longer the model — it is everything around the model: parsing, cohort
matching, calibration, evidence retrieval, and failure handling.

Third, we wanted the project to have a clearly defined success metric
that our faculty could audit. "This looks reasonable" is not a
validation strategy. The validation suite described in Chapter 6 was
designed specifically so that the scoring engine could be falsified
by a single test case — and, after several rounds of calibration,
made to pass all of them.


1.4  Sustainable Development Goal of the Project

FAIRDEAL aligns most directly with Sustainable Development Goal 8
(Decent Work and Economic Growth) and Sustainable Development Goal 10
(Reduced Inequalities) of the United Nations 2030 Agenda.

SDG 8 targets productive employment and decent working conditions for
all. Indian offer letters often contain clauses — training bonds,
90-day notice periods, unilateral intellectual-property assignment —
that, while legal, weigh asymmetrically on employees who have no easy
way to evaluate them. By surfacing these clauses, comparing them with
market norms, and generating concrete negotiation language, FAIRDEAL
tilts the information balance back toward the employee without
breaking the employer's ability to write standard agreements.

SDG 10 targets reduced inequality within and among countries. Access
to legal advice is itself an axis of inequality: candidates from
metropolitan families with lawyers in their network negotiate better
than candidates from tier-two and tier-three cities who sign the
first version placed in front of them. A free, open-source,
browser-based tool that runs on any laptop narrows this gap and is
one concrete way for students like us to contribute to goal 10.

The project also has a secondary alignment with SDG 9 (Industry,
Innovation and Infrastructure) through its use of open source
components and its public release on GitHub.

CHAPTER 2 — LITERATURE SURVEY
================================================================================

2.1  Overview of the Research Area

Legal Natural Language Processing sits at the intersection of three
active fields: document understanding, information extraction, and
domain-adapted language modelling. The last five years have seen rapid
progress on each of these axes.

Document understanding of legal contracts has moved from rule-based
parsers to transformer-based models that can classify clauses with
high precision. The LEGAL-BERT family of models proposed by
Chalkidis, Fergadiotis, Malakasiotis, Aletras, and Androutsopoulos
[1], pre-trained on twelve gigabytes of legal text, set strong
baselines for clause classification on English corporate contracts.
Information extraction has benefited from domain-specific benchmarks
such as CUAD, introduced by Hendrycks, Burns, Chen, and Ball [4],
which provides expert-annotated clause labels over 510 commercial
contracts and 41 clause categories. Domain-adapted language
modelling, finally, has been pushed forward by the general-purpose
LLM revolution, culminating in instruction-tuned models (GPT-4,
Gemini 2.0, Claude 3) that can answer arbitrary questions about a
contract when provided the full document text in the prompt.

In parallel, retrieval-augmented generation [7] has emerged as a
practical compromise between pure generative models (which hallucinate)
and pure retrieval systems (which do not reason). RAG grounds
generation in a vector store of domain content, and has become the
default pattern for enterprise question-answering applications.

Where this survey departs from much of the existing literature is in
geography. Almost all of the above work has been trained, benchmarked,
or deployed on English-language contracts drawn from US or European
jurisdictions. Indian employment contracts are structurally different:
they routinely mix currencies ("Rs. 18 lakhs per annum"), reference
the Industrial Disputes Act and Shops and Establishments Act,
embed region-specific conventions (Karnataka companies differ from
Maharashtra companies in notice-period norms), and rely heavily on
discretionary "as per company policy" language. Sharma, Gupta, and
Singh [5] were among the first to document these differences
systematically.


2.2  Existing Models and Frameworks

We surveyed seven families of systems most relevant to our problem.
They fall into four groups: (i) legal language models, (ii) annotated
datasets, (iii) commercial contract-review platforms, and (iv)
retrieval-augmented generation stacks.

LEGAL-BERT, by Chalkidis, Fergadiotis, Malakasiotis, Aletras, and
Androutsopoulos [1], is a domain-specific BERT variant pre-trained
on a large corpus of legal documents. It excels at clause
classification and legal named-entity recognition. It does not,
however, extract numeric fields such as CTC, compute market
percentiles, or produce negotiation language. Within the Indian
context, clause labels trained on US contracts transfer poorly
because the taxonomy is different (for example, LEGAL-BERT has no
concept of "training bond").

Anderson, Magruder, and Phipps [2] take a different angle. They
formalise employment-contract fairness as a statistical property —
a contract is fair at the thirtieth percentile but unfair at the
seventieth — rather than as a binary label. Their work is
theoretical and relies on expert annotation; it does not propose an
automated pipeline.

Commercial platforms (SpeedLegal, Kira, LawGeex) provide automated
clause highlighting and risk flagging. As observed by Kerikmäe,
Chochia, and Rull [3] and by Surden [6], these systems are designed
for legal professionals rather than for employees. They are also
closed-source and, for our audience, inaccessible.

The CUAD dataset by Hendrycks, Burns, Chen, and Ball [4] is the
most widely used benchmark for legal NLP. It labels 41 clause
categories across 510 US commercial contracts and is the de-facto
evaluation target for contract classification research. However,
CUAD contains zero Indian employment contracts and none of its
clause categories map to "training bond" or "notice period" as used
in Indian offer letters.

Lewis, Perez, Piktus, Petroni, Karpukhin, Goyal, Küttler, Lewis,
Yih, Rocktäschel, Riedel, and Kiela [7] introduced retrieval-
augmented generation, which combines a dense retriever with a
sequence-to-sequence generator. RAG has since become the standard
pattern for knowledge-grounded generation and is the retrieval
component we adopted.

Google's Gemini family [9] is the current generation of
general-purpose multimodal LLMs and the model we use for (a) targeted
field extraction when regex fails, (b) OCR on scanned pages via the
Vision API, and (c) optional narration of final results.

Finally, the sentence-BERT framework of Reimers and Gurevych [8]
provides the embedding backbone for our vector store. We use the
all-MiniLM-L6-v2 variant for its favourable accuracy-to-latency
trade-off on short clause text.

2.3  Limitations Identified from Literature Survey (Research Gaps)

The survey reveals four gaps that FAIRDEAL is built to address.

Gap 1 — No end-to-end system for Indian offer letters.
   No existing system combines extraction, benchmarking, scoring, and
   negotiation specifically for Indian employment contracts. Every
   candidate system either stops at classification, stops at risk
   detection, or targets a different jurisdiction.

Gap 2 — No standardised benchmark over Indian employment data.
   CUAD is US-focused and covers commercial contracts. There is no
   publicly available, annotated set of Indian offer letters on which
   a new system can be evaluated. Our validation corpus (Chapter 6)
   is a small step toward filling this gap.

Gap 3 — Scoring systems are opaque or theoretical.
   Anderson et al. define fairness statistically but stop short of an
   operational algorithm. Commercial tools produce risk scores but do
   not justify them. FAIRDEAL's five-dimensional scoring engine is
   fully specified in Chapter 3 and every component score is
   attributable to a specific input feature.

Gap 4 — Robustness under degraded input is rarely reported.
   Most legal NLP papers assume clean digital text. Our dataset
   contains scans from inkjet printers, skewed camera photographs of
   printouts, and DOCX files with broken styles. A practical system
   must handle all of these, and the literature offers very little
   guidance on how to do so end-to-end.

2.4  Research Objectives

The research objectives of this work are stated below.

    O1. To investigate techniques for robust ingestion of
        heterogeneous Indian employment contract files and the
        recovery of clean, machine-readable text from both
        digitally born documents and scanned printouts.

    O2. To formulate a hybrid information-extraction approach for
        the critical fields of an Indian offer letter that
        achieves full field coverage by combining deterministic
        pattern matching with a focused language-model fallback.

    O3. To design a market-benchmarking method for Indian salary
        and notice-period distributions that remains usable when
        cohort data is sparse, through a controlled cohort-
        broadening procedure.

    O4. To formulate and validate a multi-dimensional fairness
        scoring scheme that is deterministic, calibrated against
        domain expectations, and responsive to the presence of
        statutory violations.

    O5. To establish a reproducible experimental protocol for
        evaluating the proposed system on the dimensions of pass
        rate, ordering accuracy, determinism, inter-category
        discrimination, and feature–score correlation.

3.1.2  Functional Document

The Sprint I pipeline is invoked by a single FastAPI endpoint,
POST /api/analyze, which accepts a multipart/form-data request
containing the contract file and a metadata block (role, experience,
company type). The handler lives in backend/app/api/analyze.py and
delegates to a chain of services:

    parser_service.py          — format detection, pdfplumber / docx
    ocr_service.py             — Gemini Vision → PyMuPDF → Tesseract
    rule_extraction_service.py — 93 regex patterns, field sanitisation
    sniper_extraction_service.py — LLM fallback for missed fields
    llm_service.py             — Gemini wrapper with rate-limit handling

The function contract at each stage was fixed up front. The parser
returns a plain dict {text: str, pages: int, source: "digital" |
"ocr"}. The OCR service returns the same shape with an additional
provider field. The rule extractor returns a Pydantic model with
typed fields (Decimal for money, int for days). The sniper extractor
takes the rule extractor's output as input and, for each null field,
calls the LLM with a narrow prompt asking only for that field.

The design decision that mattered the most was making the sniper
operate per field rather than per document. A single "extract
everything" prompt was attempted early in Sprint I; it produced
inconsistent JSON, was sensitive to rate limits, and made debugging
hard. Switching to per-field prompts reduced LLM token use by
approximately 60 % and eliminated the "half-structured JSON" problem.


3.1.3  Architecture Document

The Sprint I architecture is a linear pipeline with one optional
branch (OCR) and one conditional branch (LLM sniper). Both branches
are driven by observable signals: the OCR branch fires when per-page
text density falls below an empirically chosen threshold, and the
sniper branch fires when the regex extractor returns null for a
required field.

The OCR cascade is implemented in ocr_service.py. For each PDF page
classified as scanned, the service attempts Gemini Vision first.
Gemini Vision is preferred because it handles multi-column layouts,
embedded letterheads, and light handwriting better than either
PyMuPDF text extraction or Tesseract on our test set. If the Gemini
Vision call fails or is rate-limited, the service falls back to
PyMuPDF's built-in text layer (which sometimes succeeds on PDFs that
look scanned but still carry a text layer). If both tiers fail, the
service renders the page at 300 DPI with pdf2image and runs Tesseract
as a last resort. All three paths feed into the same post-processor,
which normalises whitespace, removes OCR artifacts, and validates
text density one more time before returning.

The hybrid extraction flow is implemented as a pair of services:
rule_extraction_service.py is a 34-KB file containing 93 compiled
regular expressions grouped by field and scored by specificity.
Patterns are ordered so that the most specific form is tried first;
the first match wins. Currency normalisation is done after matching,
converting "18 LPA", "Rs. 1,800,000", "Rs. 18 Lacs", and "INR
18,00,000" into a single Decimal value. If, after the full sweep, any
required field is still null, sniper_extraction_service.py constructs
a focused prompt for that field only, sends it to Gemini 2.0 Flash
via llm_service.py, and parses the typed JSON response.

3.1.5  Sprint Retrospective

Two things went well. First, choosing Pydantic models as the
inter-service contract caught several bugs at the boundary long
before they reached the frontend; a Decimal field refusing to accept
a string is a clearer error than any runtime crash. Second, the
decision to run the sniper per field kept token cost low enough that
the entire six-contract test battery could be run on the free tier
of the Gemini API without hitting rate limits.

Two things did not go well. The first regex-only implementation
treated the contract as a flat string and matched "Rs. 18 lakhs"
inside an insurance cap clause as CTC, producing an inflated salary.
The fix required tagging each regex with a section-context
requirement (CTC patterns were only allowed to match in sections
labelled Compensation, Salary, or Remuneration). The second problem
was silent OCR failures: Tesseract occasionally returned a confident-
looking block of garbage. We added a post-OCR density check that
rejects outputs below a threshold and flags the page for manual
review; this fired exactly once during validation and did so
correctly.

    benchmark_service.py       — cohort loading, matching, percentiles
    red_flag_service.py        — rule engine over extracted + market data
    psychological_scoring.py   — five-dimensional score + calibration

The benchmarking service loads all 22 JSON files from
backend/data/market_data on startup. Each file encodes a single
cohort: a role (SDE, Analyst, HR, Marketing, Finance, Operations,
Product), a city cluster (Bangalore, Mumbai, Hyderabad, Delhi, Pune,
Tier-2, or Metro), and an experience band (0–2, 2–5, or 5–10 years).
Cohort matching starts from the most specific key; if the exact
cohort has fewer than thirty data points, the service broadens the
cohort by relaxing city first, then experience band, then role
aliases. Role aliases are defined through a 40-entry synonym table
that maps expressions like "Software Engineer", "Backend
Developer", "Full Stack Engineer", and "SDE-1" to the canonical
SDE role.

The red-flag engine is a pure-Python rule runner. It inspects the
extracted fields and the cohort output, then evaluates a set of
rules — notice period above 45 days, training bond over six months,
non-compete beyond twelve months, broad IP-assignment language,
missing provident-fund clause, and thirteen more. Each triggered rule
contributes a RedFlag object with severity (HIGH, MEDIUM, LOW), a
plain-English explanation, the triggering snippet, and an impact on
the downstream score.

The scoring engine is the most involved service of the sprint. It is
documented in detail in §3.2.3.


3.2.3  Architecture Document

The scoring engine computes a single fairness score in [0, 100]
through four deterministic steps.

Step 1 — Component scoring. Five component scores are computed
independently:

    S_salary:    non-linear piecewise interpolation of salary
                 percentile across six percentile bands, yielding
                 a score in [20, 100]. Defaults to 50 if percentile
                 is unavailable.

    S_notice:    inverted percentile mapping (shorter notice → higher
                 score), since shorter notice increases employee
                 mobility.

    S_benefits:  tiered base score based on benefit count, plus
                 quality bonuses for premium benefits (ESOPs, WFH,
                 gym, education reimbursement) and penalties for
                 missing statutory items (PF, gratuity).

    S_clauses:   starts at 100 and deducts for training bond,
                 non-compete, long probation, broad IP assignment,
                 garden leave, and termination-without-cause.

    S_legal:     starts at 100 and deducts for each statutory
                 violation detected.

Step 2 — Dynamic weighting. Base weights depend on role level.
Weights are renormalised so that they sum to one. When any legal
violation is detected, w_legal is elevated to 0.20 and the
remaining weights are proportionally reduced.

Step 3 — Weighted sum and context multiplier. The raw score is

    S_raw = Σ_i w_i · S_i

and is adjusted by a context multiplier μ that handles four
named contract archetypes:

    Unicorn contract:       μ = 1.08  (high CTC + equity + no
                            restrictive clauses)
    Golden handcuffs:       μ = 0.92  (high CTC, but long bond or
                            non-compete ≥ 12 months)
    Toxic contract:         μ = 0.85  (legal violation + high-
                            severity red flag)
    Startup rocket:         μ = 1.05  (startup category + ESOPs +
                            flexible notice)

Step 4 — Calibration and grade. A non-linear calibration function
expands the upper range (≥ 85), preserves linearity in the middle
(50–70), and compresses the lower range (< 30). The final score is
clamped to [0, 100] and mapped to grades: EXCEPTIONAL (≥ 95),
EXCELLENT (85–94), GOOD (70–84), FAIR (55–69), AVERAGE (40–54),
POOR (25–39), CRITICAL (< 25).

3.2.5  Sprint Retrospective

The hardest design decision of the sprint was not mathematical but
psychological: what should happen when a contract has a very high CTC
but an aggressive non-compete clause? An early version of the engine
penalised the non-compete so heavily that Amazon-style packages
scored below TCS-style packages. After a long discussion with our
guide, we introduced the context multiplier and added the
"golden-handcuffs" archetype. The philosophical position — a well-
paid contract with a non-compete is still objectively better than a
poorly paid contract without one — is defended in Chapter 6.

The second unexpected difficulty was percentile computation under
sparse cohorts. "Blockchain Developer in Bhubaneswar with one year
of experience" has no real data. Instead of returning nothing, we
broaden the cohort three times (city → experience → role alias) and
tag the result with a confidence tier. This preserved usability
without pretending to precision.

and searched against the store with clause_type as a hard filter. The
top three results per clause category are returned with their cosine
similarity, and are surfaced in the Evidence panel of the frontend.

fields, and the market percentile to build a prioritised playbook.
Each suggestion includes a target clause, a current value, a
recommended counter-ask, a justification, and a company-type-specific
phrasing hint (product companies respond better to compensation
benchmarking; service companies respond better to market-rate-for-

4.1  Overview of the Experimental Design

The empirical work behind FAIRDEAL was carried out under three
deliberate constraints. First, the validation set had to admit a
known answer for every contract in it; without that, no claim about
the correctness of a score is checkable. Second, the entire pipeline
had to remain runnable end to end on a single laptop, so that any
external reviewer could reproduce our numbers from a clean clone of
the repository. Third, every preprocessing step had to be either
deterministic by construction or, where an external service was
genuinely required, controllable through an explicit configuration
switch. These three constraints shaped the dataset, the preprocessing
chain, and the evaluation protocol described in the rest of this
chapter.

The remaining sections describe the four data sources that the study
relies on (a real-contract pilot, a synthetic validation corpus with
predefined ground truth, a retrieval knowledge base, and twenty-two
market cohorts), the preprocessing applied to each of them, the
hardware and software setup under which the experiments were run,
and the evaluation protocol that produced the metrics reported in
Chapter 6.

4.2.1  Real-Contract Pilot Corpus

Six anonymised Indian employment contracts collected from peers and
senior students form the real-contract pilot. The set was assembled
to span deliberately different document conditions. Two of the six
are clean digital PDF files exported from corporate HR portals; two
are DOCX files that had been forwarded by email and were lightly
edited along the way; one is a phone photograph of a printed offer
letter, where the page is slightly skewed and partially shadowed;
and the sixth is a scanner output where alternate pages have picked
up a faint colour cast from an inkjet printer. Personally identifying
fields — names, employee codes, manager names, account numbers,
addresses — were removed by hand before the file entered the project
tree. This pilot is used only for field-extraction evaluation
(Section 6, Subsection F). It is held out of scoring validation
on purpose, since the same authors who drafted the synthetic ground
truth would otherwise be in a position to grade the engine on
their own intuitions.


4.2.2  Synthetic Validation Corpus

The scoring engine is validated against a controlled corpus of
thirty contracts authored programmatically. We chose the synthetic
route over a larger real corpus for two reasons. First, the ground
truth could be encoded with full precision: the salary percentile,
notice percentile, benefit list, restrictive clauses, and role level
are written out by hand for each test case rather than recovered
from a document. Second, the experimental signal is not muddied by
extraction errors, so a failure of the scoring engine cannot be
masked by a successful upstream parse, and vice versa.

The corpus spans four company categories. Eleven service-MNC
contracts are modelled on TCS, Infosys, Wipro, HCL, Cognizant, Tech
Mahindra, Capgemini, Accenture, LTIMindtree, Mphasis, and Hexaware.
Eleven product-company contracts are modelled on Google, Microsoft,
Amazon, Flipkart, Adobe, Oracle, Samsung, Salesforce, Walmart,
Intuit, and Zoho. Six startup contracts are modelled on Razorpay,
PhonePe, CRED, Swiggy, Zomato, and Paytm. The remaining two
contracts are modelled on Deloitte and EY for the consulting
category. Annual CTC values range from Rs. 3.50 lakhs at the
entry-service end to Rs. 30.00 lakhs at the senior-product end,
covering the operational band of Indian tech-employment offers.
Each test case carries a predefined expected score range and an
expected grade list; both are stored alongside the contract inputs
in backend/app/evaluation/ground_truth.py and serve as the reference
against which scoring outputs are compared.


4.2.3  Retrieval Knowledge Base

The retrieval-augmented evidence layer draws on a knowledge base of
eighty-eight indexed contracts. The base aggregates the six
anonymised real contracts, the thirty synthetic contracts described
above, and a further fifty-two contracts collected during Sprint III
to cover the long tail of clause language. The latter group includes
additional service-MNC, product, and startup variants and was
assembled specifically so that retrieval over rare clause types —
garden leave, broad intellectual-property assignment, commission-
linked compensation — would return realistic neighbours rather than
collapsing onto a small set of duplicates. The knowledge base is
read-only at evaluation time and never feeds back into the scoring
engine; its only role is to surface evidence chunks that the user
sees alongside the score.


4.2.4  Market Benchmarking Cohorts

Market percentiles are computed against twenty-two curated cohorts
stored as JSON files under backend/data/market_data. Each file
encodes a triple of role, city cluster, and experience band — for
example "SDE / Bangalore / 0–2 years" or "Marketing / Mumbai /
2–5 years". Salary and notice-period distributions inside each
cohort were assembled from a combination of public reporting
(Glassdoor median bands and AmbitionBox), publicly available
company-reported salary ranges, and curated peer reports that were
cross-checked against at least three sources per data point. The
twenty-two cohorts cover seven role families (SDE, Analyst, HR,
Marketing, Finance, Operations, Product) across five to seven city
clusters and three experience bands, with a small set of fallback
cohorts that are used when no specific match can be located. The
cohort layout, including the synonym table that maps "Software
Engineer", "Backend Developer", "Full Stack Engineer", and "SDE-1"
to a canonical SDE role, is described in Section 3.2.2.


4.3  Dataset Preprocessing

Preprocessing is implemented as a chain of services under
backend/app/services/. Each stage is unit-tested in isolation and
exposes a fixed input/output contract through Pydantic models, so
a change at one boundary cannot silently break a later stage. The
preprocessing chain has five logical phases: format normalisation,
OCR for scanned pages, field extraction and sanitisation, clause
segmentation and embedding, and deduplication with ground-truth
annotation. We describe each in turn.


4.3.1  Format Normalisation

The first stage, in parser_service.py, detects the file format and
routes it to the appropriate text reader. PDF files are passed
through pdfplumber, which is generally faithful for digitally born
layouts and exposes the page-level text grid we need for the
density check below. DOCX files are read with python-docx;
paragraphs are walked in document order and assembled with explicit
line breaks, so that the regex layer downstream can rely on
positional information when matching section headers. The output
of this stage is a plain dictionary of the form
{text: str, pages: int, source: "digital" | "ocr"}. For DOCX inputs
the source is always "digital" because the format does not admit a
scanned variant.

A density check on the parsed text decides whether the OCR cascade
is required. If a PDF page contains fewer than a small empirically
chosen number of characters per square inch (after stripping
whitespace and page headers), the parser flags that page as scanned
and the OCR cascade takes over for it. The threshold was tuned on
the real-contract pilot — the goal was for scanned letters in the
pilot to trigger OCR while clean digital exports stayed on the fast
path — and has not been changed since. Pages that fall on the right
side of the threshold pass straight through with the source field
set to "digital".


4.3.2  OCR Pipeline for Scanned Pages

Scanned pages are processed by a three-tier cascade in
ocr_service.py. The first tier sends the rendered page image to
Google Gemini Vision through llm_service.py. Gemini Vision is
preferred at this position because, on our pilot images, it handled
multi-column layouts, embedded letterhead graphics, and faint
inkjet bands more reliably than the other two tiers. If the Gemini
call fails or is rate-limited, the service falls back to PyMuPDF,
which sometimes succeeds on PDFs that look scanned to the eye but
still carry a hidden text layer. The third tier renders the page at
300 DPI with pdf2image and runs Tesseract; this is the slowest and
least accurate path and is reserved for pages where the first two
tiers return nothing usable.

All three tiers feed into a shared post-processor that normalises
whitespace, strips obvious OCR artefacts (stray pipe characters,
isolated single letters, repeated punctuation runs), and runs a
second density check on the recovered text. If the post-processed
output still falls below the density threshold, the page is flagged
for manual review rather than being silently passed downstream.
This safeguard fired exactly once during the Sprint I pilot —
correctly — on a heavily compressed scan from an old inkjet printer
where Tesseract had returned a confident-looking block of garbage.


4.3.3  Field Extraction and Sanitisation

Extraction is hybrid by design. The deterministic layer, in
rule_extraction_service.py, holds ninety-three compiled regular
expressions grouped by field (CTC, notice period, training bond,
non-compete duration, probation, benefits) and ordered within each
group by specificity. The most specific pattern is tried first and
the first match wins. Each pattern carries a section-context
requirement that prevents, for example, a "Rs. 18 lakhs" reference
inside an insurance-cap clause from being captured as the
candidate's annual CTC. This contextualisation was added after a
miss during Sprint I, where exactly that pattern fired on a real
contract and inflated the salary by an order of magnitude.

Currency normalisation runs after matching. Indian salary
expressions appear in many forms — "18 LPA", "Rs. 1,800,000/-",
"Rs. 18 Lacs", "INR 18,00,000", "Cost to Company: 18 lakhs per
annum" — and the normaliser converts each surface form into a
single Decimal value in rupees. Unit suffixes are handled through
a small lookup table; lakhs and crores are expanded explicitly,
because these tokens appear nowhere in Western salary corpora and
are not handled by off-the-shelf currency parsers.

When the regex pass leaves a required field empty,
sniper_extraction_service.py constructs a focused prompt for that
single field and asks Gemini 2.0 Flash for a typed JSON answer. The
prompt template embeds the relevant section context (recovered
through a lightweight section locator) and a strict schema
definition; the response is parsed and validated against the
matching Pydantic model before it is allowed back into the
pipeline. Per-field prompting was chosen over a single
extract-everything prompt early in the project. The single prompt
produced inconsistent JSON, was sensitive to rate limits, and hid
bugs at the boundary. Switching to per-field prompts cut token
usage by roughly sixty percent and removed the half-structured-JSON
failure mode.


4.3.4  Clause Segmentation and Embedding

Documents that are admitted into the knowledge base go through an
additional segmentation stage in chunking_service.py. The chunker
walks the text, identifies headers and keyword boundaries, and
assigns each chunk to one of six clause categories: termination,
intellectual property, non-compete, confidentiality, compensation,
and a general catch-all. Where a header does not match the keyword
list, the chunker drops to paragraph-level granularity rather than
guessing at a category. Each chunk is then embedded with
sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2, a 384-dimensional encoder
selected for its favourable accuracy-to-latency trade-off on short
clause text. Embeddings are L2-normalised so that cosine similarity
reduces to a dot product at query time.

The chunks, their embeddings, and a small metadata payload
(contract_id, chunk_id, clause_type, filename, file_hash) are
persisted into the fairdeal_contracts collection in ChromaDB.
ChromaDB was chosen over FAISS for three practical reasons:
persistent storage works out of the box on Windows, thread-safe
singleton initialisation removes a class of cold-start race
conditions we hit in an earlier prototype, and metadata filtering
by clause_type can be expressed at query time as a where clause
rather than as a Python-side post-filter.


4.3.5  Deduplication and Ground-Truth Annotation

Every contract is fingerprinted with SHA-256 before it enters the
index. If a fingerprint already exists in the manifest, the
contract is skipped; this prevents accidental duplication when the
index is rebuilt and stops retrieval results from collapsing onto a
small set of repeated chunks. Ground truth for the synthetic
validation corpus is encoded directly in
backend/app/evaluation/ground_truth.py as an explicit dictionary
per test case — salary percentile, notice percentile, benefits
list, training bond status and amount, non-compete duration,
probation length, role level, equity flag, working-hours band, the
expected score range, and the expected grade list. Encoding the
ground truth in code rather than in a spreadsheet keeps it under
version control alongside the scoring engine, so any change to a
ground-truth value can be reviewed in a pull request the same way
a code change would be.


4.4  Experimental Setup

4.4.1  Hardware and Software Environment

All experiments were run on a Windows 10 development laptop with a
sixteen-gigabyte memory budget and a standard consumer SSD. The
backend runs on Python 3.11 with FastAPI as the web framework and
uvicorn as the ASGI server; the frontend runs on Node 20 with
Vite 5. The sentence-transformers encoder runs on CPU, because the
embedding workload at this scale (eighty-eight contracts and a few
thousand chunks) is small enough that GPU acceleration would not
change the wall-clock time materially. Gemini calls go to the
public API with the free-tier rate limits in effect; the validation
suite is built so that the LLM-dependent paths can be replaced with
cached responses for offline reproduction.


4.4.2  Scoring Engine Configuration

The psychological scoring engine in psychological_scoring.py is
parameterised by a base weight vector that depends on role level
(entry, mid, senior), a context-multiplier table for four contract
archetypes (unicorn, golden handcuffs, toxic, startup rocket), and
a calibration curve that expands the upper score range above
eighty-five and compresses the lower range below thirty. All three
configurations were locked at the start of Sprint III, after the
calibration discussions that followed the Sprint II retrospective,
and were not altered during the validation run reported in
Chapter 6. Locking the configuration in this way means that the
entire experimental result is a function of the input data, not of
incidental engine tweaks made during the validation pass.


4.4.3  Reproducibility Controls

Reproducibility is enforced at three levels. At the algorithm
level, the scoring engine is purely deterministic: every floating-
point step is identical across runs because no random sampling is
involved. At the data level, every input — test cases, market
cohorts, knowledge-base contracts, ground truth — is checked into
the repository under version control, so the exact data that
produced the reported numbers is available to a reviewer. At the
orchestration level, the validation suite is exposed through a
single endpoint, /api/evaluate/run, that processes all thirty
cases, computes the full metric set, and returns a JSON report; the
same code path is invoked when the suite is run from the command
line via the analyze CLI script. A reviewer who wants to reproduce
the numbers in Chapter 6 needs only to clone the repository,
install the dependencies, and call the endpoint.


4.5  Evaluation Protocol

The evaluation procedure is implemented in
backend/app/evaluation/evaluator.py and produces seven distinct
measurements, each described in this section. None of these
measurements are computed against the real-contract pilot — that
corpus is reserved for the field-extraction evaluation in §4.5.6 —
because the same team that wrote the ground truth would otherwise
be grading the engine on contracts they have already inspected.


4.5.1  Pass Rate Against Expected Score Ranges

For each of the thirty synthetic test cases, the scoring engine is
invoked with the encoded inputs (salary percentile, notice
percentile, benefits list, restrictive clauses, role level, and the
remaining flags) and a final fairness score is produced. A test
case is recorded as passing when the final score lies inside the
expected range stored in ground_truth.py. The aggregate metric is
the proportion of passing cases. Expected ranges are wide enough
to absorb minor calibration drift — an entry-level service contract
may carry an expected range of forty to sixty-five — but narrow
enough to falsify any score that breaks a category-level ordering,
such as a TCS entry contract scoring above a Salesforce senior
contract.


4.5.2  Determinism Verification

The scoring engine is run three times in succession against the
same input set, with the system clock changing between runs.
Pairwise comparison of the resulting score vectors is then
performed; a determinism score of one hundred percent requires
every contract to produce an identical final score across all three
runs. This guards against any inadvertent introduction of
nondeterminism in calibration, weighting, or context-multiplier
selection, all of which are easy to break by accident when an
engineer is iterating on the formula.


4.5.3  Known-Ordering Compliance

Fifteen contract pairs with unambiguous quality orderings are
encoded in KNOWN_ORDERINGS. Each pair takes the form (winner_id,
loser_id) — for example, a Salesforce senior contract at
Rs. 30 lakhs with equity, against a TCS entry contract at
Rs. 3.62 lakhs with a two-year training bond. The ordering accuracy
is the fraction of pairs in which the engine ranks the winner above
the loser. Pairs were chosen specifically to avoid ambiguity: a
domain expert presented with the two contracts side by side would
unambiguously prefer the winner.


4.5.4  Inter-Category Discrimination

Welch's t-test, which does not assume equal variance between
groups, is applied to the score distributions of pairs of company
categories. Cohen's d is reported alongside the t-statistic as an
effect-size measure. The categories of interest are product against
service, startup against service, and product against consulting.
The consulting group is small (n = 2) and is reported with the
caveat that the t-test result there is illustrative rather than
conclusive.


4.5.5  Feature–Score Correlation Analysis

Pearson correlation coefficients are computed between the final
score and four input features: salary percentile, benefits count,
notice percentile, and non-compete duration in months. The
expectation, derived from the design of the scoring engine, is that
the first two should correlate strongly and positively, the third
should correlate strongly and negatively, and the fourth should
correlate only weakly because of the confounding between high-CTC
contracts and the simultaneous presence of non-compete clauses.
Whether this expectation is met is reported in Chapter 6.


4.5.6  Field-Extraction Pilot Evaluation

The six real anonymised contracts are run through the full
ingestion and extraction pipeline. Per-field accuracy is tabulated
against the manually verified ground truth for that contract. The
metric for each field is the proportion of contracts on which the
regex layer alone produced the correct value; the LLM sniper is
treated as a backstop and reported separately, so that the
contribution of the deterministic and the model-based components
can be compared rather than conflated.


4.5.7  Latency Profiling

Wall-clock latency is measured stage by stage on a contract that
does not require OCR or sniper invocation, and a second time on a
worst-case contract where both paths fire. Mean latency per stage
is reported in milliseconds. The numbers reported are means over
five repeated runs after a warm cache; cold-start latency, where
the sentence-transformers encoder is still being loaded into
memory, is excluded because it is paid once per server start rather
than once per request.

The seven measurements above produce every metric reported in
Chapter 6 directly, with no hand-tuning between the experimental
run and the figures.

CHAPTER 7 — CONCLUSION AND FUTURE ENHANCEMENT
================================================================================

The FAIRDEAL project set out to solve a narrow but important
problem: give an Indian job candidate a quick, trustworthy,
data-backed second opinion on the offer letter in front of them.
Over three sprints we built the ingestion, extraction, benchmarking,
scoring, and retrieval components, wrapped them in a FastAPI
backend and a React dashboard, and validated the whole thing
against a controlled 30-contract corpus and a six-contract real-
world pilot.

The validation confirmed that the scoring engine behaves
predictably: a 100 % pass rate against predetermined score ranges,
100 % determinism across repeated runs, and 100 % known-ordering
compliance on fifteen unambiguous contract pairs. Feature–score
correlations (salary r = 0.934, benefits r = 0.943, notice
r = -0.753) confirm the engine responds to the features it should
respond to, in the directions a domain expert would expect. Inter-
category discrimination was large (Cohen's d = 4.60 between product
and service companies), matching the candidate-level experience of
choosing between these two types of offers. On the pilot of six
real Indian contracts, regex extraction achieved 100 % accuracy on
CTC and probation, with the hybrid regex-plus-LLM pipeline covering
every remaining gap by design.

The project has obvious limitations. The scoring validation uses a
synthetic 30-contract corpus rather than a large annotated real
corpus; the real-world pilot of six contracts is too small to
support strong generalisation claims. The system is English-only
today. Market benchmarking precision degrades in niche roles where
cohort data is sparse. Training-bond valuation is fixed at a point
estimate rather than being modelled probabilistically over the
bond duration. These are honest limitations and each feeds into the
future-work list below.

Future enhancements. Five directions are planned.

First, multi-language support. Hindi, Telugu, Tamil, Bengali, and
Marathi are common languages for employment paperwork in non-
metropolitan India. Adding them requires language detection on
parse, model routing (a multilingual sentence transformer for
embeddings, a multilingual LLM for sniper extraction), and
region-specific red-flag rules.

Second, live market data. The current 22 cohorts are curated and
updated manually. Integrating one or two public job-portal APIs
would keep percentiles current and allow us to publish confidence
intervals that reflect the actual data volume per cohort.

Third, knowledge-base expansion. The current 88-contract base is
sufficient for retrieval but small in absolute terms. We intend to
grow it past 500 contracts, potentially partnering with student
groups and career-services offices to collect anonymised offer
letters ethically.

Fourth, negotiation outcome learning. Right now the playbook is
rule-based. With a consenting user base, we could log which
negotiation asks succeeded and which did not, and over time learn
company-specific priors for the probability that each ask is
accepted.

Fifth, legal-compliance expansion. The current legal dimension
checks a short list of statutory thresholds. A deeper integration
with Indian labour-law databases (the Code on Wages 2019, Shops
and Establishments Act, Industrial Disputes Act) would allow
jurisdiction-specific cross-checks and flag more subtle
non-compliance patterns.

FAIRDEAL is open source and lives at https://github.com/
(repository URL to be confirmed before submission). Everything
described in this report is reproducible from a fresh clone.
