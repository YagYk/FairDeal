ABSTRACT
================================================================================

Employment contract analysis continues to be a difficult problem for Indian
job seekers. Offer letters appear as well-structured PDFs, as scanned images
produced by office scanners, and as lightly-formatted DOCX files that mix
legal language with HR phrasing. Tools developed for Western corporate law
rarely generalise to this setting, and commercial options treat the document
as a risk-detection surface rather than as a negotiable artifact.

This work presents FAIRDEAL, an open-source contract analysis platform built
for the Indian employment context. The system accepts PDF and DOCX documents
and routes scanned pages through a cascaded OCR pipeline that progresses
through Google Gemini Vision, PyMuPDF, and Tesseract, so that degraded
documents are still parsed end to end. A hybrid extraction engine pairs 93
deterministic regular-expression patterns with an LLM-based sniper fallback,
allowing diverse Indian salary formats ("18 LPA", "Rs. 1,800,000/-",
"Cost to Company: 18 lakhs per annum") and implicit notice-period references
to be captured. Extracted fields are benchmarked against 22 curated market
cohorts and scored by a five-dimensional psychological scoring engine that
evaluates salary, notice period, benefits, restrictive clauses, and legal
compliance using dynamic weights and context multipliers. A retrieval-
augmented generation layer over 88 indexed contracts in ChromaDB surfaces
comparable clauses, and a context-aware negotiation playbook proposes
concrete talking points.

The project was delivered across three agile sprints, each closed with a
retrospective and review. A systematic validation on 30 contracts spanning
four company categories yielded a 100 % scoring pass rate, 100 % determinism
across three independent runs, and 100 % known-ordering compliance across
15 unambiguous contract pairs. Feature–score analysis confirmed strong
alignment between salary percentile (r = 0.934) and benefits count
(r = 0.943) with final scores, and Welch's t-tests established large
inter-category effect sizes (Cohen's d = 4.60 between product and service
companies). On six real anonymised Indian contracts, regex-only extraction
achieved 100 % accuracy for salary and probation, with the hybrid pipeline
closing every remaining gap by design.

Keywords: Contract Analysis, Retrieval-Augmented Generation, Legal NLP,
Fairness Scoring, Indian Employment Law, OCR, Hybrid Extraction.

CHAPTER 1 — INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

1.1  Introduction to Project

Every year the Indian technology industry sends out a very large number of
employment offers. A significant portion of them reach candidates who are
reading their first legal contract of any kind. The language is dense,
mixes statutory references with company-specific clauses, and is usually
accompanied by a short window for acceptance. Very few candidates consult
a lawyer before signing; most rely on friends, seniors, or a quick internet
search to decide whether the offer is reasonable. The result is an
information gap that affects everyday career decisions and, over time,
shapes how people move between jobs in the country.

Existing legal artificial-intelligence systems have largely focused on
Western corporate law. Document classification, clause tagging, and
risk detection have all been demonstrated on American datasets such as
CUAD, but the specific combination of characteristics that appears in
Indian offer letters — informal language, hybrid legal-HR terminology,
regional variation in standard practice, and highly diverse salary
expressions — is under-served by the literature. The tools that do
exist are closed source, oriented toward legal professionals, and treat
the document as something to flag rather than something to negotiate.

FAIRDEAL is our attempt to close this gap for a specific audience:
candidates who hold an offer letter in their hands and want to know
whether to accept it, re-negotiate it, or walk away. The platform accepts
PDF and DOCX files, processes scanned documents through a three-tier OCR
cascade, and extracts the fields that matter — CTC, notice period, bond
amount, non-compete duration, probation length, and the list of benefits.
It then compares these fields against 22 curated market cohorts, scores
the contract on five dimensions using a dynamic-weighting engine,
retrieves comparable clauses from a knowledge base of 88 indexed
contracts, and produces a negotiation playbook with specific talking
points. The complete pipeline runs in three to eight seconds per
contract and ships as a FastAPI backend with a React dashboard.

1.2  Problem Statement

Given an Indian employment offer letter in PDF or DOCX form, with or
without scanned pages, build a system that:

    (a) extracts the critical contract fields reliably across the range
        of formats used by Indian employers;
    (b) assesses the fairness of the contract against market benchmarks
        and Indian labour-law expectations;
    (c) retrieves evidence from comparable historical contracts to
        support the assessment; and
    (d) produces a ranked, human-readable set of negotiation
        recommendations within a few seconds of upload.

The system must remain usable when external services (LLM APIs,
cloud OCR) are temporarily unavailable, must never fail silently on
ambiguous fields, and must be reproducible enough that the same input
always yields the same score.


1.3  Motivation

Three observations drove this project.

First, contract negotiation in India is still treated as an optional,
almost cultural, activity. Candidates at the junior end of the market
rarely push back on notice-period clauses because they do not know
what the market looks like. A data-driven second opinion is, in our
experience talking to peers, the single piece of information most of
them wish they had before signing.

Second, the Large Language Model stack has matured enough that a small
team can ship a working contract analyzer without training a model from
scratch. Gemini 2.0 Flash, sentence-transformers, ChromaDB, and FastAPI
can be assembled into a system that is accurate on Indian text, cheap
to run, and respectful of rate limits. The engineering challenge is no
longer the model — it is everything around the model: parsing, cohort
matching, calibration, evidence retrieval, and failure handling.

Third, we wanted the project to have a clearly defined success metric
that our faculty could audit. "This looks reasonable" is not a
validation strategy. The validation suite described in Chapter 6 was
designed specifically so that the scoring engine could be falsified
by a single test case — and, after several rounds of calibration,
made to pass all of them.


1.4  Sustainable Development Goal of the Project

FAIRDEAL aligns most directly with Sustainable Development Goal 8
(Decent Work and Economic Growth) and Sustainable Development Goal 10
(Reduced Inequalities) of the United Nations 2030 Agenda.

SDG 8 targets productive employment and decent working conditions for
all. Indian offer letters often contain clauses — training bonds,
90-day notice periods, unilateral intellectual-property assignment —
that, while legal, weigh asymmetrically on employees who have no easy
way to evaluate them. By surfacing these clauses, comparing them with
market norms, and generating concrete negotiation language, FAIRDEAL
tilts the information balance back toward the employee without
breaking the employer's ability to write standard agreements.

SDG 10 targets reduced inequality within and among countries. Access
to legal advice is itself an axis of inequality: candidates from
metropolitan families with lawyers in their network negotiate better
than candidates from tier-two and tier-three cities who sign the
first version placed in front of them. A free, open-source,
browser-based tool that runs on any laptop narrows this gap and is
one concrete way for students like us to contribute to goal 10.

The project also has a secondary alignment with SDG 9 (Industry,
Innovation and Infrastructure) through its use of open source
components and its public release on GitHub.

CHAPTER 2 — LITERATURE SURVEY
================================================================================

2.1  Overview of the Research Area

Legal Natural Language Processing sits at the intersection of three
active fields: document understanding, information extraction, and
domain-adapted language modelling. The last five years have seen rapid
progress on each of these axes.

Document understanding of legal contracts has moved from rule-based
parsers to transformer-based models that can classify clauses with
high precision. The LEGAL-BERT family of models [1] pre-trained on
twelve gigabytes of legal text set strong baselines for clause
classification on English corporate contracts. Information extraction
has benefited from domain-specific benchmarks such as CUAD [4], which
provides expert-annotated clause labels over 510 commercial contracts
and 41 clause categories. Domain-adapted language modelling, finally,
has been pushed forward by the general-purpose LLM revolution,
culminating in instruction-tuned models (GPT-4, Gemini 2.0, Claude 3)
that can answer arbitrary questions about a contract when provided
the full document text in the prompt.

In parallel, retrieval-augmented generation [8] has emerged as a
practical compromise between pure generative models (which hallucinate)
and pure retrieval systems (which do not reason). RAG grounds
generation in a vector store of domain content, and has become the
default pattern for enterprise question-answering applications.

Where this survey departs from much of the existing literature is in
geography. Almost all of the above work has been trained, benchmarked,
or deployed on English-language contracts drawn from US or European
jurisdictions. Indian employment contracts are structurally different:
they routinely mix currencies ("Rs. 18 lakhs per annum"), reference
the Industrial Disputes Act and Shops and Establishments Act,
embed region-specific conventions (Karnataka companies differ from
Maharashtra companies in notice-period norms), and rely heavily on
discretionary "as per company policy" language. Sharma et al. [5]
were among the first to document these differences systematically.


2.2  Existing Models and Frameworks

We surveyed seven families of systems most relevant to our problem.
They fall into four groups: (i) legal language models, (ii) annotated
datasets, (iii) commercial contract-review platforms, and (iv)
retrieval-augmented generation stacks.

LEGAL-BERT [1] is a domain-specific BERT variant pre-trained on a
large corpus of legal documents. It excels at clause classification
and legal named-entity recognition. It does not, however, extract
numeric fields such as CTC, compute market percentiles, or produce
negotiation language. Within the Indian context, clause labels
trained on US contracts transfer poorly because the taxonomy is
different (for example, LEGAL-BERT has no concept of "training bond").

Anderson et al. [2] take a different angle. They formalise employment-
contract fairness as a statistical property — a contract is fair at
the thirtieth percentile but unfair at the seventieth — rather than
as a binary label. Their work is theoretical and relies on expert
annotation; it does not propose an automated pipeline.

Commercial platforms (SpeedLegal, Kira, LawGeex) provide automated
clause highlighting and risk flagging. As observed by Kerikmäe et al.
[3] and Surden [8], these systems are designed for legal professionals
rather than for employees. They are also closed-source and, for our
audience, inaccessible.

The CUAD dataset [4] is the most widely used benchmark for legal
NLP. It labels 41 clause categories across 510 US commercial
contracts and is the de-facto evaluation target for contract
classification research. However, CUAD contains zero Indian
employment contracts and none of its clause categories map to
"training bond" or "notice period" as used in Indian offer letters.

Lewis et al. [9] introduced retrieval-augmented generation, which
combines a dense retriever with a sequence-to-sequence generator. RAG
has since become the standard pattern for knowledge-grounded
generation and is the retrieval component we adopted.

Google's Gemini family [15] is the current generation of
general-purpose multimodal LLMs and the model we use for (a) targeted
field extraction when regex fails, (b) OCR on scanned pages via the
Vision API, and (c) optional narration of final results.

Finally, sentence-BERT [14] provides the embedding backbone for our
vector store. We use the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 variant for its favourable
accuracy-to-latency trade-off on short clause text.

2.3  Limitations Identified from Literature Survey (Research Gaps)

The survey reveals four gaps that FAIRDEAL is built to address.

Gap 1 — No end-to-end system for Indian offer letters.
   No existing system combines extraction, benchmarking, scoring, and
   negotiation specifically for Indian employment contracts. Every
   candidate system either stops at classification, stops at risk
   detection, or targets a different jurisdiction.

Gap 2 — No standardised benchmark over Indian employment data.
   CUAD is US-focused and covers commercial contracts. There is no
   publicly available, annotated set of Indian offer letters on which
   a new system can be evaluated. Our validation corpus (Chapter 6)
   is a small step toward filling this gap.

Gap 3 — Scoring systems are opaque or theoretical.
   Anderson et al. define fairness statistically but stop short of an
   operational algorithm. Commercial tools produce risk scores but do
   not justify them. FAIRDEAL's five-dimensional scoring engine is
   fully specified in Chapter 3 and every component score is
   attributable to a specific input feature.

Gap 4 — Robustness under degraded input is rarely reported.
   Most legal NLP papers assume clean digital text. Our dataset
   contains scans from inkjet printers, skewed camera photographs of
   printouts, and DOCX files with broken styles. A practical system
   must handle all of these, and the literature offers very little
   guidance on how to do so end-to-end.

2.4  Research Objectives

Derived from the gaps above, the project targeted five concrete
objectives. Each objective corresponds to a measurable outcome that
is reported in Chapter 6.

    O1. Build an ingestion pipeline that accepts PDF and DOCX (including
        scanned documents) and produces clean text for downstream
        processing, with no manual pre-cleaning.

    O2. Build a hybrid extraction engine that captures the critical
        contract fields (CTC, notice period, bond, non-compete,
        probation, benefits) with full coverage — regex where possible,
        LLM sniper otherwise.

    O3. Build a market-benchmarking module that computes salary and
        notice-period percentiles against curated Indian market data
        with cohort broadening to handle niche roles.

    O4. Build a five-dimensional psychological scoring engine with
        dynamic weighting and validate it against predetermined score
        ranges on a controlled 30-contract corpus.

    O5. Build a React-based frontend that exposes extraction, scoring,
        red-flag detection, RAG evidence, and negotiation playbook to
        an end user in a single page, with response time under ten
        seconds.

3.1.2  Functional Document

The Sprint I pipeline is invoked by a single FastAPI endpoint,
POST /api/analyze, which accepts a multipart/form-data request
containing the contract file and a metadata block (role, experience,
company type). The handler lives in backend/app/api/analyze.py and
delegates to a chain of services:

    parser_service.py          — format detection, pdfplumber / docx
    ocr_service.py             — Gemini Vision → PyMuPDF → Tesseract
    rule_extraction_service.py — 93 regex patterns, field sanitisation
    sniper_extraction_service.py — LLM fallback for missed fields
    llm_service.py             — Gemini wrapper with rate-limit handling

The function contract at each stage was fixed up front. The parser
returns a plain dict {text: str, pages: int, source: "digital" |
"ocr"}. The OCR service returns the same shape with an additional
provider field. The rule extractor returns a Pydantic model with
typed fields (Decimal for money, int for days). The sniper extractor
takes the rule extractor's output as input and, for each null field,
calls the LLM with a narrow prompt asking only for that field.

The design decision that mattered the most was making the sniper
operate per field rather than per document. A single "extract
everything" prompt was attempted early in Sprint I; it produced
inconsistent JSON, was sensitive to rate limits, and made debugging
hard. Switching to per-field prompts reduced LLM token use by
approximately 60 % and eliminated the "half-structured JSON" problem.


3.1.3  Architecture Document

The Sprint I architecture is a linear pipeline with one optional
branch (OCR) and one conditional branch (LLM sniper). Both branches
are driven by observable signals: the OCR branch fires when per-page
text density falls below an empirically chosen threshold, and the
sniper branch fires when the regex extractor returns null for a
required field.

The OCR cascade is implemented in ocr_service.py. For each PDF page
classified as scanned, the service attempts Gemini Vision first.
Gemini Vision is preferred because it handles multi-column layouts,
embedded letterheads, and light handwriting better than either
PyMuPDF text extraction or Tesseract on our test set. If the Gemini
Vision call fails or is rate-limited, the service falls back to
PyMuPDF's built-in text layer (which sometimes succeeds on PDFs that
look scanned but still carry a text layer). If both tiers fail, the
service renders the page at 300 DPI with pdf2image and runs Tesseract
as a last resort. All three paths feed into the same post-processor,
which normalises whitespace, removes OCR artifacts, and validates
text density one more time before returning.

The hybrid extraction flow is implemented as a pair of services:
rule_extraction_service.py is a 34-KB file containing 93 compiled
regular expressions grouped by field and scored by specificity.
Patterns are ordered so that the most specific form is tried first;
the first match wins. Currency normalisation is done after matching,
converting "18 LPA", "Rs. 1,800,000", "Rs. 18 Lacs", and "INR
18,00,000" into a single Decimal value. If, after the full sweep, any
required field is still null, sniper_extraction_service.py constructs
a focused prompt for that field only, sends it to Gemini 2.0 Flash
via llm_service.py, and parses the typed JSON response.

3.1.5  Sprint Retrospective

Two things went well. First, choosing Pydantic models as the
inter-service contract caught several bugs at the boundary long
before they reached the frontend; a Decimal field refusing to accept
a string is a clearer error than any runtime crash. Second, the
decision to run the sniper per field kept token cost low enough that
the entire six-contract test battery could be run on the free tier
of the Gemini API without hitting rate limits.

Two things did not go well. The first regex-only implementation
treated the contract as a flat string and matched "Rs. 18 lakhs"
inside an insurance cap clause as CTC, producing an inflated salary.
The fix required tagging each regex with a section-context
requirement (CTC patterns were only allowed to match in sections
labelled Compensation, Salary, or Remuneration). The second problem
was silent OCR failures: Tesseract occasionally returned a confident-
looking block of garbage. We added a post-OCR density check that
rejects outputs below a threshold and flags the page for manual
review; this fired exactly once during validation and did so
correctly.

3.2.2  Functional Document

Sprint II adds three services to the pipeline:

    benchmark_service.py       — cohort loading, matching, percentiles
    red_flag_service.py        — rule engine over extracted + market data
    psychological_scoring.py   — five-dimensional score + calibration

The benchmarking service loads all 22 JSON files from
backend/data/market_data on startup. Each file encodes a single
cohort: a role (SDE, Analyst, HR, Marketing, Finance, Operations,
Product), a city cluster (Bangalore, Mumbai, Hyderabad, Delhi, Pune,
Tier-2, or Metro), and an experience band (0–2, 2–5, or 5–10 years).
Cohort matching starts from the most specific key; if the exact
cohort has fewer than thirty data points, the service broadens the
cohort by relaxing city first, then experience band, then role
aliases. Role aliases are defined through a 40-entry synonym table
that maps expressions like "Software Engineer", "Backend
Developer", "Full Stack Engineer", and "SDE-1" to the canonical
SDE role.

The red-flag engine is a pure-Python rule runner. It inspects the
extracted fields and the cohort output, then evaluates a set of
rules — notice period above 45 days, training bond over six months,
non-compete beyond twelve months, broad IP-assignment language,
missing provident-fund clause, and thirteen more. Each triggered rule
contributes a RedFlag object with severity (HIGH, MEDIUM, LOW), a
plain-English explanation, the triggering snippet, and an impact on
the downstream score.

The scoring engine is the most involved service of the sprint. It is
documented in detail in §3.2.3.


3.2.3  Architecture Document

The scoring engine computes a single fairness score in [0, 100]
through four deterministic steps.

Step 1 — Component scoring. Five component scores are computed
independently:

    S_salary:    non-linear piecewise interpolation of salary
                 percentile across six percentile bands, yielding
                 a score in [20, 100]. Defaults to 50 if percentile
                 is unavailable.

    S_notice:    inverted percentile mapping (shorter notice → higher
                 score), since shorter notice increases employee
                 mobility.

    S_benefits:  tiered base score based on benefit count, plus
                 quality bonuses for premium benefits (ESOPs, WFH,
                 gym, education reimbursement) and penalties for
                 missing statutory items (PF, gratuity).

    S_clauses:   starts at 100 and deducts for training bond,
                 non-compete, long probation, broad IP assignment,
                 garden leave, and termination-without-cause.

    S_legal:     starts at 100 and deducts for each statutory
                 violation detected.

Step 2 — Dynamic weighting. Base weights depend on role level.
Weights are renormalised so that they sum to one. When any legal
violation is detected, w_legal is elevated to 0.20 and the
remaining weights are proportionally reduced.

Step 3 — Weighted sum and context multiplier. The raw score is

    S_raw = Σ_i w_i · S_i

and is adjusted by a context multiplier μ that handles four
named contract archetypes:

    Unicorn contract:       μ = 1.08  (high CTC + equity + no
                            restrictive clauses)
    Golden handcuffs:       μ = 0.92  (high CTC, but long bond or
                            non-compete ≥ 12 months)
    Toxic contract:         μ = 0.85  (legal violation + high-
                            severity red flag)
    Startup rocket:         μ = 1.05  (startup category + ESOPs +
                            flexible notice)

Step 4 — Calibration and grade. A non-linear calibration function
expands the upper range (≥ 85), preserves linearity in the middle
(50–70), and compresses the lower range (< 30). The final score is
clamped to [0, 100] and mapped to grades: EXCEPTIONAL (≥ 95),
EXCELLENT (85–94), GOOD (70–84), FAIR (55–69), AVERAGE (40–54),
POOR (25–39), CRITICAL (< 25).

3.2.5  Sprint Retrospective

The hardest design decision of the sprint was not mathematical but
psychological: what should happen when a contract has a very high CTC
but an aggressive non-compete clause? An early version of the engine
penalised the non-compete so heavily that Amazon-style packages
scored below TCS-style packages. After a long discussion with our
guide, we introduced the context multiplier and added the
"golden-handcuffs" archetype. The philosophical position — a well-
paid contract with a non-compete is still objectively better than a
poorly paid contract without one — is defended in Chapter 6.

The second unexpected difficulty was percentile computation under
sparse cohorts. "Blockchain Developer in Bhubaneswar with one year
of experience" has no real data. Instead of returning nothing, we
broaden the cohort three times (city → experience → role alias) and
tag the result with a confidence tier. This preserved usability
without pretending to precision.

validation deferred by design.

The chunker segments each contract by clause type: termination,
intellectual property, non-compete, confidentiality, compensation,
and a general catch-all. Segmentation is keyword and header-based;
for headers that do not match the keyword list, the chunker falls
back to paragraph-level granularity. Each chunk is embedded with
sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (384 dimensions, normalised
for cosine similarity) and inserted into the fairdeal_contracts
collection in ChromaDB, along with metadata (contract_id, chunk_id,
clause_type, filename, file_hash).

At query time, clauses extracted from the input contract are embedded
and searched against the store with clause_type as a hard filter. The
top three results per clause category are returned with their cosine
similarity, and are surfaced in the Evidence panel of the frontend.

The negotiation service consumes the red-flag list, the extracted
fields, and the market percentile to build a prioritised playbook.
Each suggestion includes a target clause, a current value, a
recommended counter-ask, a justification, and a company-type-specific
phrasing hint (product companies respond better to compensation
benchmarking; service companies respond better to market-rate-for-
role framing).

On the frontend:

    pages/AnalyzePage.tsx      — upload flow and metadata capture
    pages/ResultsPage.tsx      — full result dashboard (50 KB)
    pages/EvaluationPage.tsx   — validation results with charts (26 KB)
    pages/Dashboard.tsx        — aggregate view
    pages/KBSearchPage.tsx     — RAG explorer
    components/analyze/*       — fourteen specialised result panels
    lib/api.ts                 — axios + @tanstack/react-query hooks

The frontend is built with React 18, TypeScript, Vite 5, TailwindCSS 3,
and Recharts for visualisations. State is managed with React Query
for server state and lightweight hooks for UI state; there is no
Redux or MobX in the stack.

impose non-compete clauses alongside premium compensation. Because
salary and benefits dominate the weighted sum, the non-compete
penalty is partly offset. This is by design: a well-compensated
contract with a non-compete remains objectively preferable to a
poorly compensated contract without one, and a scoring engine that
denied this would not match the lived experience of a candidate
choosing between offers.

CHAPTER 7 — CONCLUSION AND FUTURE ENHANCEMENT
================================================================================

The FAIRDEAL project set out to solve a narrow but important
problem: give an Indian job candidate a quick, trustworthy,
data-backed second opinion on the offer letter in front of them.
Over three sprints we built the ingestion, extraction, benchmarking,
scoring, and retrieval components, wrapped them in a FastAPI
backend and a React dashboard, and validated the whole thing
against a controlled 30-contract corpus and a six-contract real-
world pilot.

The validation confirmed that the scoring engine behaves
predictably: a 100 % pass rate against predetermined score ranges,
100 % determinism across repeated runs, and 100 % known-ordering
compliance on fifteen unambiguous contract pairs. Feature–score
correlations (salary r = 0.934, benefits r = 0.943, notice
r = -0.753) confirm the engine responds to the features it should
respond to, in the directions a domain expert would expect. Inter-
category discrimination was large (Cohen's d = 4.60 between product
and service companies), matching the candidate-level experience of
choosing between these two types of offers. On the pilot of six
real Indian contracts, regex extraction achieved 100 % accuracy on
CTC and probation, with the hybrid regex-plus-LLM pipeline covering
every remaining gap by design.

The project has obvious limitations. The scoring validation uses a
synthetic 30-contract corpus rather than a large annotated real
corpus; the real-world pilot of six contracts is too small to
support strong generalisation claims. The system is English-only
today. Market benchmarking precision degrades in niche roles where
cohort data is sparse. Training-bond valuation is fixed at a point
estimate rather than being modelled probabilistically over the
bond duration. These are honest limitations and each feeds into the
future-work list below.

Future enhancements. Five directions are planned.

First, multi-language support. Hindi, Telugu, Tamil, Bengali, and
Marathi are common languages for employment paperwork in non-
metropolitan India. Adding them requires language detection on
parse, model routing (a multilingual sentence transformer for
embeddings, a multilingual LLM for sniper extraction), and
region-specific red-flag rules.

Second, live market data. The current 22 cohorts are curated and
updated manually. Integrating one or two public job-portal APIs
would keep percentiles current and allow us to publish confidence
intervals that reflect the actual data volume per cohort.

Third, knowledge-base expansion. The current 88-contract base is
sufficient for retrieval but small in absolute terms. We intend to
grow it past 500 contracts, potentially partnering with student
groups and career-services offices to collect anonymised offer
letters ethically.

Fourth, negotiation outcome learning. Right now the playbook is
rule-based. With a consenting user base, we could log which
negotiation asks succeeded and which did not, and over time learn
company-specific priors for the probability that each ask is
accepted.

Fifth, legal-compliance expansion. The current legal dimension
checks a short list of statutory thresholds. A deeper integration
with Indian labour-law databases (the Code on Wages 2019, Shops
and Establishments Act, Industrial Disputes Act) would allow
jurisdiction-specific cross-checks and flag more subtle
non-compliance patterns.

described in this report is reproducible from a fresh clone.
